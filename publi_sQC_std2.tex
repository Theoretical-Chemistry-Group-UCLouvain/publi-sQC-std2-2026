\section{Introduction}

The accurate theoretical characterization of electronic excited states and response properties in complex molecular environments remains a central challenge in computational chemistry.  
Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) is widely used for medium-sized systems, yet its formal $O(N^4)$ scaling and known limitations in the treatment of charge-transfer, Rydberg, and doubly excited states typically restrict routine applications to a few hundred atoms.
As research increasingly targets biomolecular assemblies, supramolecular aggregates, and functional materials, methods capable of combining predictive accuracy with favorable scaling have become essential. \cite{burschBestPracticeDFTProtocols2022}

Simplified quantum chemistry (sQC) methods have emerged as an effective route to bridge the longstanding gap between predictive accuracy and computational feasibility.\cite{bannwarthExtendedTightbindingQuantum2021,loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
Their development was largely motivated by the cost of modern \textit{ab initio} wave-function approaches and TD-DFT, which, despite their improved reliability, remained restricted to relatively small systems.  
Prior to their widespread adoption, semiempirical techniques constituted the primary tools for modeling excited states in large molecules.\cite{thielSemiempiricalQuantumChemical2014}
These methodologies frequently relied on the zero-differential overlap (ZDO) approximation.
Among these, the ZINDO/S method\cite{ridleyIntermediateNeglectDifferential1973,zernerSemiempiricalMolecularOrbital1991} (Intermediate Neglect of Differential Overlap using Configuration Interaction with Singles) proved particularly successful for describing vertical excitations and electronic spectra, enabling early studies of complex chromophores and biological assemblies.  
Methods based on Neglect of Diatomic Differential Overlap\cite{dewarGroundStatesMolecules1977,dewarDevelopmentUseQuantum1985} (NDDO) with orthogonalization corrections, including the OM$x$ families,\cite{kolbMNDOModelMethodical1993} further improved the exploration of potential energy surfaces and excited-state dynamics.  

The modern simplified framework was inaugurated with the introduction of the simplified Tamm–Dancoff approximation (sTDA) in 2013,\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013} followed shortly by its TD-DFT counterpart (sTD-DFT).\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
These methods achieve substantial acceleration through controlled approximations, including the neglect of the exchange–correlation kernel (see below).  
The approach was subsequently extended to ultra-large systems through the sTD-DFT-xTB variant,\cite{grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016} enabling explicit quantum-mechanical excited-state calculations for systems containing several thousand atoms.  
Alternative schemes inspired by the success of sTD-DFT/sTDA have also been proposed to further accelerate excited-state calculations.
The TD-DFT+TB method introduced by Rüger \emph{et al.}\cite{rugerTightbindingApproximationsTimedependent2016} combines a DFT ground state with a monopole approximation and local exchange--correlation functionals, delivering speedups of up to two orders of magnitude while maintaining errors below $\sim$0.15~eV for excitation energies in large plasmonic systems.
Giannone and Della Sala later proposed the TD-DFT-as approach,\cite{giannoneMinimalAuxiliaryBasis2020} based on a resolution-of-identity scheme with a single $s$-type auxiliary function per atom, from which sTD-DFT, sTDA, and TD-DFT+TB can be viewed as approximations; this strategy was subsequently extended to hybrid functionals through TD-DFT-ris.\cite{zhouMinimalAuxiliaryBasis2023}
Furthermore, both sTDA and TD-DFT-ris can serve as preconditionner for actual full TD-DFT calculation, resulting in non-negligible speep-ups.\cite{zhouAcceleratingMolecularProperty2021,zhouConvergingTimeDependentDensity2024}
Additional developments include the extension of TD-DFT+TB to analytical excited-state gradients, the incorporation of periodic boundary conditions within sTDA,\cite{hehnExcitedStatePropertiesExtended2022} and the introduction of simplified GW/BSE approaches,\cite{choSimplifiedGWBSE2022} collectively illustrating the sustained effort to reduce computational cost while preserving predictive accuracy.

Over time, the sTD framework has expanded to encompass a broad spectrum of linear and nonlinear response properties,\cite{dewergifossePerspectiveSimplifiedQuantum2021} such as polarizabilities, hyerpolarizabilities,\cite{dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2018} two-photon absor\-ption,\cite{dewergifosseUltrafastEvaluationTwoPhoton2022} excited-state absorption,\cite{dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2019} optical rotation,\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2020} and spin-flip formulations for strongly correlated situations,\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedSpinFlipTimeDependent2019}  thereby transforming simplified methods into versatile tools for large-scale excited-state simulations.  
Recent developments have led to the eXact-integral simplified TD-DFT (XsTD-DFT) methodology,\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} which moves the simplified framework toward a more rigorous first-principles formulation.  
By replacing semiempirical integral approximations with exact one- and two-center atomic-orbital integrals, XsTD-DFT removes the dependence on fitted parameters while preserving favorable scaling.  
The extension to support range-separated hybrid functionals\cite{dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} further improves the description of long-range charge-transfer excitations and nonlinear optical responses through the solution of the full Casida equations.  
Taken together, these advances establish simplified TD-DFT as a reliable platform for predictive simulations of very large molecular systems.  

These capabilities are implemented in the open-source \texttt{std2} program (formerly distributed as \texttt{stda}), which provides a modular environment for computing excited states together with a broad range of linear and nonlinear response properties, including one- (1PA) and two-photon absorption (2PA) spectra, electronic circular dichroism (ECD), optical rotation (OR), second-harmonic generation (SHG), and excited-state absorption (ESA).  
In this article, we present an overview of the capabilities of \texttt{std2}, beginning with a concise description of the underlying theoretical framework in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, followed by benchmark assessments and representative applications that illustrate its performance across diverse spectroscopic observables and system sizes.  

The availability of robust and computationally efficient methodologies now makes it possible to explicitly target: \begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item large conformational ensembles, and/or  
	\item large systems and realistic molecular environments at the fully quantum-mechanical level.\cite{loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
\end{inparaenum}
Continuum solvation models, such as PCM, neglect specific solute–environment interactions, whereas multiscale strategies like QM/MM introduce dependencies on force fields, boundary definitions, and polarization treatments.  
All-atom quantum mechanical (AQM) strategies therefore provide an appealing alternative by treating the entire system at the QM level, avoiding locality assumptions while naturally capturing some long-range effects.  
Within this framework, two complementary paradigms have emerged (Fig.~\ref{fig:aqm}): the All-Atom Single-structure QM (ASQM) and the All-Atom Dynamic QM (ADQM) approaches. \cite{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}
ASQM evaluates response properties for a representative structure and is particularly effective for rigid systems whose optical signatures are only weakly affected by structural fluctuations.  
By contrast, ADQM incorporates conformational sampling—typically through molecular dynamics trajectories or Boltzmann-weighted ensembles—thereby accounting for thermal disorder and environment-induced spectral broadening.  
Together, ASQM and ADQM define a unified strategy for predictive spectroscopy of large molecular systems, enabling the level of structural realism to be matched to the physical complexity of the problem; these approaches constitute the focus of Section~\ref{sec:illu}.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

In this section, the (X)sTD-DFT theory will be explained. Unless otherwise mentioned, $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$,... refer to molecular orbitals (MOs), $i$, $j$,... to occupied, $a$, $b$,... to unoccupied ones, $\ket{m}$,... to electronic excited states, and $\mu$, $\nu$,... to atomic orbitals (AOs). 
Atomic units should be assumed throughout.

\subsection{Basics of (TD)-DFT}

Solving the electronic Schrödinger equation constitutes the foundational step of \emph{ab initio} quantum chemistry. 
In density functional theory (DFT), this problem is reformulated in terms of the one-electron density, $\rho(\vec{r})$, rather than the many-electron wavefunction. 
The density is defined as the marginal probability distribution obtained by integrating the squared norm of the wavefunction, $\Psi$, over all position and spin coordinates except one,
\begin{equation}
	\rho(\vec{r}) = N \int \cdots \int d\omega_1\, d\vec{x}_2 \ldots d\vec{x}_{N}\, 
	\left|\Psi(\{\vec{x}_i\})\right|^2,	\qquad 
	N = \int d\vec{r}\, \rho(\vec{r}),
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total number of electrons.
Within the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, the two Hohenberg-Kohn theorems~\cite{hohenbergInhomogeneousElectronGas1964} provide the conceptual foundation of DFT:
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item the ground-state energy is a unique functional of the electron density, $E[\rho]$, and all physical observables are likewise determined by $\rho$; and
	\item the variational principle holds for any trial density, ensuring that $E[\rho] \ge E[\rho_0]$, where $\rho_0$ is the exact ground-state density.
\end{inparaenum}
Together, these results establish a direct route to the optimal density and, consequently, to all ground-state properties.
In practice, the total energy functional is decomposed as
\begin{equation*}
	E[\rho] = E_{NN} + V_{\mathrm{ext}}[\rho] + F_{HK}[\rho], 
	\qquad 
	F_{HK}[\rho] = T[\rho] + E_{ee}[\rho],
\end{equation*}
where $E_{NN}$ is the nuclear-nuclear repulsion, $T[\rho]$ the exact kinetic energy, and $E_{ee}[\rho]$ the electron-electron interaction energy. The explicit form of $F_{HK}$, however, is unknown. A central difficulty is the kinetic-energy term $T[\rho]$, for which no exact density-based expression exists. Kohn and Sham~\cite{kohnSelfConsistentEquationsIncluding1965} addressed this by introducing an auxiliary noninteracting reference system, described by a single Slater determinant, with kinetic energy $T_S[\rho]$. The remaining contributions are absorbed into the exchange-correlation functional, $E_{XC}[\rho]$.
The resulting Kohn-Sham (KS) energy functional reads
\begin{equation*}
	F_{KS}[\rho] = T_S[\rho] + J[\rho] + E_{XC}[\rho],
\end{equation*}
where $J[\rho]$ is the classical Coulomb term. 
The exchange-correlation (XC) potential, $\hat{V}_{XC}(\vec  r)= \frac{\partial E_{XC}}{\partial\rho(\vec r)}$, is obtained from an approximate XC functional (XCF), the accuracy of which ultimately determines the performance of a KS calculation. Over the past decades, many XCFs have been proposed, including hybrid and range-separated hybrid forms that incorporate a fraction of exact Hartree-Fock exchange, improving accuracy across diverse chemical systems~\cite{burkeAdiabaticConnectionMethod1997,yangGeneralizedAdiabaticConnection1998,yanaiNewHybridExchange2004}.
Finally, invoking the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) ansatz leads to the familiar matrix representation of the KS equations:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{F}_{KS}\,\mathbf{C} 
	= \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_{KS}$ is the KS matrix, $\mathbf{S}$ the overlap matrix, $\mathbf{C}$ the coefficient matrix, and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ the diagonal matrix of KS orbital energies.

Then, one is generally interested in quantities associated with measurable physical phenomena. 
When such quantities cannot be obtained directly as expectation values of appropriate operators, molecular properties are defined as the response of the system to one or several external perturbations, through perturbation theory. 
For example: 
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item a change in molecular geometry ($\Delta \vec{x}$) is linked to the molecular gradient (first order) and to harmonic vibrational frequencies derived from the molecular Hessian (second order),
	\item the application of a time-dependent magnetic field $\vec{B}(t)$ yields properties such as optical rotation (via the electric-magnetic polarizability, $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$) and NMR shieldings, or
	\item the application of a time-dependent electric field $\vec{F}(t)$ gives access to the polarizability ($\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$), the first hyperpolarizability ($\dstack{\beta}{\dstack{\leftrightarrow}{\leftarrow}}$), and related quantities.
\end{inparaenum}
In passing, when an electromagnetic field is applied, the induced dipole moment is given by the following Taylor expansion:\begin{equation}
	\vec\mu_{ind} = \dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}\cdot\vec{F} + \frac{1}{2}\dstack{\beta}{\dstack{\leftrightarrow}{\leftarrow}}:\vec{F}\vec{F}+ \dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} + \ldots
\end{equation}

However, the Hohenberg-Kohn and Kohn-Sham theorems are valid only for stationary (time-independent) situations. 
Fortunately, Runge and Gross~\cite{rungeDensityFunctionalTheoryTimeDependent1984} extended DFT to the time-dependent domain by proving that the time-dependent electron density uniquely determines the time-dependent external potential, thereby generalizing the first Hohenberg-Kohn theorem. 
Furthermore, van Leeuwen~\cite{vanleeuwenCausalitySymmetryTimeDependent1998} demonstrated that Kohn-Sham orbitals satisfy the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. 
The adiabatic approximation is then commonly invoked, meaning that the time-dependent XC potential is approximated by evaluating the ground-state XC functional at the instantaneous density:
\begin{equation}
	\hat V_{XC}(\vec{r}, t) \approx 
	\frac{\partial E_{XC}[\rho]}{\partial \rho(\vec{r}, t)}. \label{eq:adiabatic}
\end{equation}

In practice, both linear and nonlinear response functions can thus be obtained analytically, and we refer to Refs. \citenum{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995} and \citenum{parkerQuadraticResponseProperties2018} for an in-depth introduction. 
In time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT), for a time-dependent perturbation operator $\hat V$ oscillating at frequency $\omega$ and polarized in direction $\zeta$, and assuming real orbitals, the linear response vectors are obtained by solving:
\begin{equation}
	\left[
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\
		\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}
	\end{pmatrix}
	- \omega
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{0} \\
		\mathbf{0} & -\mathbf{1}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\right]
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X}_\zeta(\omega) \\
		\mathbf{Y}_\zeta(\omega)
	\end{pmatrix}
	=
	-\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{V}_\zeta \\
		\mathbf{V}_\zeta^\star
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
where the elements of $\mathbf{V}_\zeta$ and $\mathbf{V}_\zeta^\star$ are given by ${V}_{\zeta, ia} = \langle i | \hat{V} | a \rangle$ and ${V}_{\zeta, ia}^\star = \langle a | \hat{V} | i \rangle$. 
These vectors collect the off-diagonal matrix elements of $\langle p | \hat{V} | q \rangle$ corresponding to excitation ($i \to a$) and de-excitation ($a \to i$) processes. 
The vectors $\mathbf{X}_\zeta(\omega)$ and $\mathbf{Y}_\zeta(\omega)$ therefore describe the excitation and de-excitation components of the linear response, respectively.
The matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ constitute the electronic Hessian (supermatrices). 
They are indexed using the occupied-virtual notation ($ia$, $jb$), indicating that each element involves one occupied and one unoccupied orbital. 
Assuming a global hybrid functional of the form $E_{XC} = (1-a_x)\,E_X^{\mathrm{GGA}} + a_x\,E_X^{\mathrm{HF}} + E_C^{\mathrm{GGA}}$, their elements, obtained via the Slater-Condom rules, are:
\begin{align}
	A_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 2\,(ia|jb)
	- a_x\,(ij|ab)
	+ (1-a_x)\,(ia|f_{XC}|jb),
	\nonumber \\
	B_{ia,jb} &= 2\,(ia|bj)
	- a_x\,(ib|aj)
	+ (1-a_x)\,(ia|f_{XC}|bj),
	\label{eq:TD-AandB}
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_i$ and $\varepsilon_a$ denote occupied and virtual orbital energies, respectively, and $a_x$ is the fraction of nonlocal Fock exchange. 
The terms $(ia|jb)$, $(ia|bj)$, and $(ib|aj)$ denote two-electron exchange integrals, while $(ij|ab)$ denotes a Coulomb-type integral. 
The terms $(ia|f_{XC}|jb)$ and $(ia|f_{XC}|bj)$ represent exchange-correlation kernel contributions (deriving from Eq.~\eqref{eq:adiabatic}). 
In the Tamm-Dancoff approximation (TDA)~\cite{hirataTimedependentDensityFunctional1999}, the $\mathbf{B}$ matrix is neglected.

Because the poles of the response function correspond to electronic excitation energies, it is common to set $\mathbf{V}_\zeta = 0$, yielding the non-hermitian eigenvalue problem:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\
		\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X} \\
		\mathbf{Y}
	\end{pmatrix}
	=
	\omega_m
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{0} \\
		\mathbf{0} & -\mathbf{1}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X} \\
		\mathbf{Y}
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:casida}
\end{equation}
which is known as the Casida equation~\cite{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995}. 
More precisely, Eq.~\eqref{eq:casida} is a symplectic eigenvalue problem~\cite{furcheAcceleratingMolecularProperty2016}, where each positive eigenvalue $\omega_m$ corresponds to a transition energy from the ground state $\ket{0}$ to an excited state $\ket{m}$. 
The associated amplitude vectors $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ describe, respectively, the excitation and de-excitation components of each transition.

Once the excitation or response vectors are computed, they can be assembled into the desired property tensors (see below). 
In practice, this is done via the computation of the $\mathbf A \pm \mathbf B$ matrices, rather than the $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$ matrices themselves.\cite{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995,bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014,furcheAcceleratingMolecularProperty2016,parkerQuadraticResponseProperties2018}
For large systems, however, the dimensions of the $\mathbf{A} \pm \mathbf{B}$ matrices and the cost of computing their elements rapidly become prohibitive. 
To address this issue and provide a more efficient alternative that balances computational cost and accuracy, \citeauthor{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013} introduced in 2013 a simplified variant of TD-DFT within the TDA framework, known as simplified TDA (sTDA)~\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013}. 
One year later, the same simplifications were extended to the full TD-DFT formalism, yielding the sTD-DFT method~\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}, which was recently extended to yield XsTD-DFT\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024}. 
This is the topic of the next subsection.

\subsection{The simplified TD-DFT framework}

In essence, simplified TD-DFT methods achieve substantial reductions in computational cost through three key approximations, while maintaining sufficient accuracy for a wide range of applications: 
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item the excitation space is truncated by retaining only the most relevant configuration state functions (CSFs),
	\item one-electron integrals are evaluated exactly, whereas two-electron integrals are approximated using the zero-differential-overlap (ZDO) framework, with different treatments of the electron–repulsion integrals (ERIs) defining the various simplified TD-DFT variants, and
	\item all exchange–correlation functional (XCF) contributions to the response equations are neglected.
\end{inparaenum}

As a consequence, each (X)sTD-DFT calculation proceeds through the following sequence of steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:computationalsequence}): 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Define the active molecular-orbital space as 
	$\varepsilon_p \in [\varepsilon_{\mathrm{LUMO}} - E'_w,\; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HOMO}} + E'_w]$, 
	where $E'_w = 2\,(1 + 0.8\,a_x)\,E_w$.\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
	\item From this active space, identify the primary CSFs (P-CSFs) $\ket{\Psi_i^a}$ that satisfy $A'_{ia,ia} < E_{\mathrm{thr}}$.  
	For CSFs $\ket{\Psi_j^b}$ with $A'_{jb,jb} > E_{\mathrm{thr}}$, generate a secondary set (S-CSFs) by retaining those for which the perturbative energy contribution $E^{(2)}_{jb}$ exceeds a threshold $E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{thr}}$.  
	This perturbative measure quantifies the cumulative second-order influence of an S-CSF $\ket{\Psi_j^b}$ on all selected P-CSFs:
	\begin{equation*}
		E^{(2)}_{jb} 
		= \sum_{ia \in \mathrm{P\text{-}CSFs}} 
		\frac{|A'_{ia,jb}|^2}{A'_{jb,jb} - A'_{ia,ia}}.
	\end{equation*}
	The elements $A'_{ia,jb}$ are computed using approximate expressions (see below).  
	All remaining CSFs are discarded.  
	This selection strategy is known as the \emph{single-threshold} scheme, though alternative approaches have been proposed.\cite{beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021}
	\item Construct the reduced supermatrices $\mathbf{A'} \pm \mathbf{B'}$ (or only $\mathbf{A'}$ in the TDA), representing a subset of the full matrices, using the selected CSFs and the approximate expressions for their elements.
	\item Solve for the excitation or response vectors using the chosen simplified TD-DFT formalism.
	\item Assemble the relevant property tensors, noting that XCF contributions remain neglected at this level.
\end{enumerate}

Two simplified TD-DFT variants are examined in this work: the recently introduced XsTD-DFT approach,\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} and the original sTD-DFT formalism.\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}  

\subsubsection{XsTD-DFT}

The XsTD-DFT method was specifically designed to overcome several shortcomings of sTD-DFT, most notably its limited reliability for systems with substantial long-range exchange or pronounced charge-transfer character.  
XsTD-DFT assumes a range-separated hybrid (RSH) XCFs,\cite{dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} and constructs the approximate TD-DFT supermatrix elements by removing all explicit XCF contributions. The resulting expressions are:
\begin{align}
	(\mathbf A'+ \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &=  \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 4\,(ia|jb)' - b_x\,[(ib|aj)'+(ij|ab)'] \nonumber\\&\hspace{2em}- c_x\,[(ij|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|ab)'+ (ib|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|aj)'],\nonumber\\
	(\mathbf A'-\mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)+  b_x\,[(ib|aj)'  - (ij|ab)'] \nonumber\\&\hspace{2em}-c_x\,[(ij|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|ab)'-(ib|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|aj)'] . 
	\label{eq:xstddft}
\end{align}
Here, $b_x$ and $c_x$ denote the constant and long-range fractions of Hartree–Fock exchange, respectively, and $\omega$ is the RSH range-separation parameter.

The approximate form of the $(ia|jb)'$ integrals follows from a sequence of controlled simplifications.  
We begin with the general expression of the bielectronic integrals in the MO basis,
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) = \sum_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} C_{i\mu}^\star C_{a\alpha} C^\star_{j\nu} C_{b\beta}\,(\mu\alpha|\nu\beta),
\end{equation*}
where $(\mu\alpha|\nu\beta)$ denotes a four-center two-electron AO integral.  
Introducing the Löwdin-orthogonalized coefficients $\mathbf{C}^\perp = \mathbf{S}^{1/2}\mathbf{C}$ gives
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) = \sum_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}( C_{i\mu}^\perp)^\star C_{a\alpha}^\perp (C^\perp_{j\nu})^\star C_{b\beta}^\perp\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\alpha|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\beta),
\end{equation*}
where each orthogonalized AO is written as $\lambda_\mu = \sum_\beta \phi_\beta\,S_{\mu\beta}^{-1/2}$, with $\{\phi_\beta\}$ the original atomic basis functions.
The ZDO approximation then replaces the four-center integrals by\begin{equation*}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\alpha|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\beta) \approx \delta_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\nu\beta}\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu),
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) \approx (ia|jb)' = \sum_{\mu\nu}( C_{i\mu}^\perp)^\star C_{a\mu}^\perp (C^\perp_{j\nu})^\star C_{b\nu}^\perp\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu).
\end{equation*}
The remaining two-center quantity is, by definition,
\begin{align*}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu)
	= \sum_{\alpha\beta\kappa\eta} (\alpha\beta|\kappa\eta)\,
	S^{-1/2}_{\mu\alpha}S^{-1/2}_{\mu\beta}S^{-1/2}_{\nu\kappa}S^{-1/2}_{\nu\eta}.
\end{align*}
The leading contributions are:
\begin{align*}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu) &= 
	(\mu\mu|\nu\nu)\, (S^{-1/2}_{\mu\mu})^2(S^{-1/2}_{\nu\nu})^2 
   \\
  &\hspace{1em}+2\sum_{\alpha\neq\mu}(\mu\alpha|\nu\nu)\,S^{-1/2}_{\mu\mu} S^{-1/2}_{\mu\alpha} (S^{-1/2}_{\nu\nu})^2 + 2\sum_{\alpha\neq\nu} (\mu\mu|\nu\alpha)\, (S^{-1/2}_{\mu\mu})^2S^{-1/2}_{\nu\nu}S^{-1/2}_{\nu\alpha}\\
  &\hspace{1em}+ \ldots,
	%+ (\mu\mu|\mu\mu)\, (S^{-1/2}_{\mu\mu})^2(S^{-1/2}_{\nu\mu})^2 + (\nu\nu|\nu\nu)\,(S^{-1/2}_{\mu\nu})^2 (S^{-1/2}_{\nu\nu})^2  + \ldots
\end{align*}
with $S^{-1/2}_{\mu\mu}\geq  1 \land S^{-1/2}_{\nu\nu} \geq 1$, while $|S^{-1/2}_{\mu\alpha}| \ll 1 \land |S^{-1/2}_{\nu\alpha}| \ll 1$ (and generally negative).
So, to first approximation, $(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu) \approx (\mu\mu|\nu\nu)$ \textbf{[ce qui veut dire qu'au final, on approxime que $S^{-1/2}_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$, donc que les orbitales atomiques sont isolées. Je sais pas ce que ça veut dire, mais c'est drôle]}.
Introducing the transition charges $q^{ia}_\mu = \big(C^{\perp}_{\mu i}\big)^\star C^{\perp}_{\mu a}$ finally leads to
\begin{equation}
	(ia|jb)' = \sum_{\mu\nu} q^{ia}_\mu\,q^{jb}_\nu\,(\mu\mu|\nu\nu). \label{eq:xsints}
\end{equation}




\subsubsection{sTD-DFT}

In contrast, the sTD-DFT model retains the global hybrid form of the XCF (Eq.~\eqref{eq:TD-AandB}).  
The approximate electronic Hessian elements take the form\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013,bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
\begin{align}
	(\mathbf A'+ \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 4\,(ia|jb)'_K - (ij|ab)'_J - a_x\,(ib|aj)'_K, \nonumber\\
	(\mathbf A' - \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i) - (ij|ab)'_J + a_x\,(ib|aj)'_K.
	\label{eq:stddft}
\end{align}
Contrary to Eq.~\eqref{eq:xsints}, these integrals are evaluated through a atomic monopole-based representation, with the transition charges compressed on atoms:
\begin{equation}
	(ia|jb)'_K = \sum_{AB} q_A^{ia}\,q_B^{jb}\,(AA|BB)_K, 
	\qquad 
	q_A^{ia} = \sum_{\mu \in A} \big(C^{\perp}_{\mu i}\big)^\star C^{\perp}_{\mu a},
	\label{eq:sints}
\end{equation}
where the sums now run over atomic centers $A$ and $B$.  
The required two-center terms $(AA|BB)$ are evaluated with Mataga–Nishimoto–Ohno–Klopman (MNOK) damped Coulomb operators.\cite{klopmanSemiempiricalTreatmentMolecular1964}  
For Coulomb-type contributions,
\begin{equation*}
	(AA|BB)_J =
	\left[\frac{1}{R_{AB}^{\gamma_J} + \left(a_x\,\eta_{AB}\right)^{-\gamma_J}}\right]^{1/\gamma_J},
\end{equation*}
where $R_{AB}$ is the interatomic distance, $\eta_{AB} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_A + \eta_B)$ is the average chemical hardness, and $\gamma_J$ is a global damping parameter.  
Exchange-type integrals employ
\begin{equation*}
	(AA|BB)_K =
	\left[\frac{1}{R_{AB}^{\gamma_K} + \eta_{AB}^{-\gamma_K}}\right]^{1/\gamma_K}.
\end{equation*}

Note, however, that because the explicit evaluation of all $(pq|rs)'$ terms via Eq.~\eqref{eq:xsints} is significantly more demanding in XsTD-DFT than the corresponding sTD-DFT expressions via Eq.~\eqref{eq:sints}, Ref.~\citenum{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} recommends a hybrid workflow.  
In this strategy, CSF screening is performed using sTD-DFT expressions [Eq.~\eqref{eq:stddft}], while the final $\mathbf{A}'\pm\mathbf{B}'$ supermatrices are constructed using the more accurate XsTD-DFT formulation [Eq.~\eqref{eq:xstddft}].  
Parameter sets ensuring consistency between the screening stage and the final XsTD-DFT matrices are provided in Ref.~\citenum{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} for several XCF.

\subsection{Obtaining and interpreting responses}

Initially developed for UV/Vis and ECD spectroscopy, the simplified TD-DFT framework has since been extended to a broad range of linear and quadratic molecular response properties, and  their residues.\cite{dewergifossePerspectiveSimplifiedQuantum2021,loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
Formally, any linear response function admits a sum-over-states representation:\cite{orrPerturbationTheoryNonlinear1971}
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A; \hat B}}_{\omega} = \sum_{\ket{m}} \frac{\braket{0|\hat A|m}\braket{m|\hat B|0}}{\omega-\omega_m} - \frac{\braket{0|\hat B|m}\braket{m|\hat A|0}}{\omega+\omega_m}.
	\label{eq:soslin}
\end{equation}
For $\hat A = \hat B = \hat\mu$ ($\hat\mu$ is the electric dipole operator), Eq.~\eqref{eq:soslin} yields the electric dipole polarizability $\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$, whereas choosing $\hat A = \hat\mu$ and $\hat B = \hat m$ ($\hat m$ is the magnetic moment operator) gives the electric–magnetic dipole polarizability $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$.
Using the response vectors defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:response}, the same quantity can be recast as
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = Tr[A_\zeta \gamma^{(\pm)}_\eta(\omega)] = 2\,\sum_{ia} A_{\zeta,ia}\,\underbrace{[X_{\eta, ia}(\omega) \pm Y_{\eta,ia}(\omega)]}_{\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta,ia}(\omega)},
	\label{eq:linresp}
\end{equation}
where the occupied–virtual elements of $\mathbf A_\zeta$ are given by $\braket{i|\hat A_\zeta|a}$, and $\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega)$ defines the frequency-dependent transition density matrix ($\omega$PDM).  
For real operators $\hat A$ such as $\hat\mu$, the $+$ branch is used to obtain the relevant tensor components of $\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$ and $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$.

Residues of Eq.~\eqref{eq:soslin} yield the ground-to-excited-state transition moments,
\begin{equation*}
	\lim_{\omega\to\omega_m} (\omega-\omega_m)\braket{\braket{\hat A; \hat B}}_{\omega} = \braket{0|\hat A|m}\braket{m|\hat B|0},
\end{equation*}
which can be evaluated directly from the amplitude vectors:
\begin{equation}
	A_{\zeta}^{0m} = \braket{0|A_\zeta|m} = \sqrt{2}\,\sum_{ia} \mu_{\zeta,ia}\underbrace{[X_{ia} \pm Y_{ia}]}_{\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m,ia}}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m}$ corresponds to the one-electron transition density matrix (1TDM), with $\gamma^{(+)}_{0m}$ used for UV/Vis intensities ($\vec\mu_{0m}$) and $\gamma^{(-)}_{0m}$ for ECD rotational strengths ($\vec m_{0m}$).

To enable chemically transparent interpretation, both the linear response and its residues may be expressed in terms of natural response orbitals (NROs)\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} or, in the resonant limit, natural transition orbitals (NTOs).\cite{dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019}  
Within the NTO framework, the 1TDM admits a compact singular-value decomposition,
\begin{equation*}
	\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m} = \mathbf U\Lambda_{0m}\mathbf V^T,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf U$ and $\mathbf V$ define the hole and particle orbital subspaces, respectively, and $\Lambda_{0m} = \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\sqrt{\lambda_2},\ldots)$.  
The set $\{\lambda_i\}$ provides the weight of each NTO, and the effective number of contributing pairs is quantified via the participation ratio:
\begin{equation}
	PR_{NTO} = \frac{[\sum_i\lambda_i]^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2}.
\end{equation}
Typically, only a small number of NTO pairs dominate, providing a chemically intuitive picture of the excitation.

Analogously, the frequency-dependent $\omega$PDM can be compressed into NROs\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} via
\begin{equation*}
	\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega) = \mathbf U\,\Lambda_\eta\,\mathbf V^T,
\end{equation*}
leading to
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = 2 \sum_i \Upsilon_{\zeta,i}\Lambda_{ \eta,i}(\omega) = \sum_i R_{\zeta\eta, i}(\omega),
\end{equation}
with $\Upsilon_\zeta = \mathbf U^T \mathbf A_\zeta \mathbf V$.  
The weight of each NRO contribution is given by
\begin{equation*}
	w_i(\omega) = \frac{|R_{\zeta\eta,i}(\omega)|}{\sum_i R_{\zeta\eta, i}(\omega)},
\end{equation*}
and the corresponding participation ratio reads
\begin{equation}
	PR_{NRO}(\omega)  = \frac{[\sum_i w_i(\omega)]^2}{\sum_i w_i(\omega)}.
\end{equation}
An alternative perspective is obtained by applying a Löwdin orthogonalization to $\mathbf A$ and the 1TDM.  
Starting from $\mathbf C = \mathbf P \Sigma \mathbf Q^T$, one defines
\begin{equation*}
	\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\zeta} = (\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T) \mathbf A_\zeta(\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)^T
	\quad\text{and}\quad
	\tilde{\Lambda}_{\eta}(\omega) = (\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)[\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega)](\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)^T,
\end{equation*}
such that
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = 2 \sum_{\mu\nu} \tilde\Upsilon_{\zeta,\mu\nu}\tilde\Lambda_{ \eta,\mu\nu}(\omega) = \sum_{\mu\nu} \tilde R_{\zeta\eta, \mu\nu}(\omega).
\end{equation}
This formulation decomposes the response into atomic-orbital contributions, which may be further aggregated over chemically meaningful fragments.  
The resulting site (AA) and inter-fragment (AB) terms are given by
\begin{equation*}
	\Omega_{\zeta\eta}^{AB}(\omega) = 2\sum_{\mu\in A,\nu\in B} \tilde R_{\zeta\eta, \mu\nu}(\omega).
\end{equation*}
Together, these two complementary decompositions constitute the RespA analysis protocol,\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} enabling concise and chemically interpretable descriptions of both static and frequency-dependent response phenomena.


\subsection{Implementation details}

The methods discussed in the previous section are implemented in the \texttt{std2} executable (currently version~2.0), freely available on GitHub (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/std2}). 
The program is written in Fortran and uses \texttt{libcint}\cite{libcint} for the evaluation of atomic-orbital integrals, while linear-algebra routines are delegated to either netlib's BLAS/LAPACK or Intel's oneAPI MKL, to be chosen at compilation times. 
The most computationally demanding modules are parallelized using OpenMP.

The executable works as a command-line input (CLI) program, whose options are documented in the GitHub repository (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/std2/blob/master/std2_manual.pdf}).

Every (X)sTD-DFT calculation requires a ground-state wavefunction as input. 
For this purpose, \texttt{std2} reads MOLDEN-formatted files, which are produced by most quantum-chemistry packages.
If needed, external tools such as Multiwfn\cite{luMultiwfnMultifunctionalWavefunction2012} can also convert wavefunction information into MOLDEN format.
For very large systems, one of the main application domains of \texttt{std2},\cite{beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021,lescosSelfassemblingStructureNonlinear2021,loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025} obtaining a sufficiently accurate ground-state wavefunction at a standard DFT level may be challenging or prohibitively costly. 
To address this, the auxiliary program \texttt{xtb4stda} (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/xtb4stda}) has been developed.\cite{grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016}
It generates a wavefunction at the xTB level using a specially optimized augmented basis set that includes an additional set of polarization functions, significantly improving the description of the virtual orbital space, which is traditionally a weakness of xTB methods. 
We expect the upcoming g-xTB approach\cite{froitzheimGxTBGeneralPurposeExtended2025} explicitly designed to remedy deficiencies in the HOMO-LUMO gap and virtual space to further enhance the quality of such starting wavefunctions.


\section{Illustrations}\label{sec:illu}


\subsection{Excitations: 1PA and ECD spectra}

sTD-DFT was originally developed to reduce the computational cost of 1PA and ECD spectra calculations while retaining the essential physics of TD-DFT.\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013,bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014,bannwarthElectronicCircularDichroism2015,grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016,bannwarthElectronicCircularDichroism2016} 
The recently introduced XsTD formalism further improves accuracy by restoring key integral contributions at limited additional cost.\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} 
Establishing the reliability of these low-cost approaches is therefore a prerequisite before extending their use to realistic, large-scale systems.
Reference~\citenum{maraldiEvaluatingPerformanceExact2025} evaluates the performance of TD, sTD, and XsTD methods for 1PA spectra using several hybrid and range-separated hybrid exchange--correlation functionals, benchmarking against RI-CC2 results obtained with the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set for the first bright excitation ($f_{0\to n} > 0.02$). 
Results for the CT (QUEST) dataset are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-ct} (Fig.~S1). 
These datasets comprise small- to medium-sized push--pull $\pi$-conjugated molecules dominated by valence excitations, often with pronounced charge-transfer character. 
XsTD-DFT closely tracks conventional TD-DFT for both excitation energies ($\Delta E_{0\to n}$) and oscillator strengths ($f_{0\to n}$), as reflected by comparable mean deviations (MDs) and mean absolute deviations (MADs). 
In particular, XsCAM-B3LYP shows excellent agreement with RI-CC2 across both datasets. 
By contrast, sTD-DFT exhibits larger errors, especially with functionals containing high fractions of Hartree--Fock exchange (e.g., BHHLYP and M06-2X).

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./Figure2}

Results are shown for several XCFs and levels of approximation (TD, sTD, and XsTD). Left: excitation energies. Right: oscillator strengths. For each method, the mean deviation of the difference is reported, with the MAD given in parentheses.}
	\label{fig:1pa-ct}
\end{figure}

Overall, XsTD-DFT emerges as a robust low-cost alternative to TD-DFT, reducing CPU time by roughly two to three orders of magnitude while maintaining similar accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-timing}). 
The XsTDA approximation, which provides additional computational savings, likewise reproduces TDA-DFT trends with good fidelity.\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024}
Both sTD and XsTD nonetheless inherit a limitation of ZDO-based methods, namely the inadequate description of $\pi\to n^\star$ and $d\to d$ excitations.\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} 
The former is partially improved within XsTD, likely through favorable error compensation.\cite{maraldiEvaluatingPerformanceExact2025} 
This behavior suggests that a more rigorous treatment of exchange integrals, akin to the INDO framework, could further enhance predictive accuracy.

\textbf{[...  and there is spin-flip]} \cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedSpinFlipTimeDependent2019,dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019,kostadinovaSingletFissionTinkertoys2022}

The computation of excitation spectra has recently been extended to near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (NEXAFS), further broadening the applicability of simplified excited-state methods.\cite{dewergifosseUltrafastNearEdgeXray2025}
For chlorophyll~A (Fig.~\ref{fig:nexafs}) , the carbon K-edge spectrum computed at the XsSRC2/TDA/cc-pCVDZ level reproduces the experimental lineshape with remarkable fidelity, despite a small systematic energy shift.  
The main absorption band is dominated by two intense transitions near 285~eV, in close agreement with the experimental maximum, demonstrating that core--valence excitations of complex chromophores can be described at modest computational cost.  
Notably, 632 excited states involving more than 3000 basis functions are obtained within seconds, highlighting the efficiency of the approach.  
The scalability of the method is further illustrated by the PPG$_{10}$ collagen model comprising 600 atoms.  
Its oxygen K-edge spectrum, constructed from more than 85\,000 excited states, shows excellent agreement with experiment across a broad energy window.  
The first absorption band is accurately reproduced in both position and width, with the most intense transition lying within \SI{0.6}{\electronvolt} of the experimental maximum and corresponding to a localized $1s_{\mathrm O}\rightarrow n/\pi^\star$ excitation on the peptide bond.  
Higher-energy features are likewise captured once appropriate spectral broadening is applied, reflecting the dense manifold of short-lived states above the ionization threshold.

The demonstrated accuracy and efficiency of sQC approaches make it possible to address a longstanding challenge in computational spectroscopy: the realistic simulation of molecules embedded in complex environments. 
Beyond the electronic structure method itself, reliable spectra require accounting for environmental effects, structural dynamics, and, when necessary, vibronic contributions. 
Flavin mononucleotide (FMN), discussed in Ref.~\citenum{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}, offers a stringent test case for these methodologies (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-fmn}). 
Gas-phase RI-CC2 and sTD-DFT-xTB spectra are remarkably similar and correctly reproduce the first experimental absorption band, validating the use of sTD-DFT-xTB as a reliable low-cost approach. 
Both methods, however, strongly blue-shift the second band, underscoring the decisive role of the aqueous environment. Incorporating implicit solvent within ASQM or ADQM-Boltz. schemes improves the first excitation energy but fails to recover relative intensities and overall spectral shape; Boltzmann averaging produces only minor changes compared to a single optimized structure, indicating that conformational diversity alone is insufficient.
In contrast, the ADQM-MD protocol, which explicitly includes solvent molecules and dynamical fluctuations, yields striking agreement with experiment without empirical energy shifts. 
The positions, intensities, and broadening of the main absorption bands are accurately reproduced once a sufficiently large solvation sphere is employed. 
These findings demonstrate that the FMN spectrum is governed by specific solute--solvent interactions and dynamic effects inaccessible to static or implicit models. 


\begin{figure}[!h]
	\centering
	\fbox{Mix Figure 18 and 19 of Ref.~\citenum{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}}
	\caption{Experimental 1PA spectra of FMN recorded in water in comparison with ASQM and ADQM methodologies.}
	\label{fig:1pa-fmn}
\end{figure}

The ADQM protocol is also essential for describing the ECD spectra of flexible biomolecules, as it explicitly accounts for structural fluctuations.  
This is illustrated by myoglobin in Ref.~\citenum{seibertBiomolecularStructureInformation2017}, a prototypical member of the globin family responsible for intramuscular oxygen transport, comprised roughly 2500 atoms, therefore far beyond the reach of conventional excited-state quantum-chemical methods.  
Within the ADQM framework, performed at the sTDA-xTB level, however, its CD spectrum was obtained by averaging over 100 snapshots extracted from a molecular dynamics trajectory, thereby suppressing artificial spectral features and yielding a physically meaningful lineshape.  
The resulting spectrum  (Fig.~\ref{fig:ecd-myo}) reproduces all characteristic experimental signatures with very good agreement.  
Owing to the presence of eight $\alpha$-helices, the CD response is dominated by the backbone transitions typical of helical peptides, confirming that the method correctly captures secondary-structure fingerprints while simultaneously accounting for the heme cofactor and its coupling to the protein environment.

	\centering
	\fbox{Fig.~2 of Ref.~\citenum{seibertBiomolecularStructureInformation2017}}
	\caption{Calculated CD spectra of myoglobin computed (blue solid line).}
	\label{fig:ecd-myo}
\end{figure}

By contrast, for more rigid structures, the ASQM protocol already provides a reliable description of optical properties without the need for extensive configurational averaging.  
The photoactive yellow protein (PYP), a 1931-atom system, exemplifies this regime: it can be treated at the XsCAM-B3LYP/TDA level using mixed basis sets, making fully quantum-mechanical calculations tractable even on modest computational resources, as discussed in Ref.~\citenum{dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024}.  
Excitation energies and oscillator strengths of the first transition are well reproduced even with relatively small basis sets, provided that diffuse and polarization functions are included on the chromophore.  
The computed 1PA and ECD spectra (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa+ecd-pyp}) show striking agreement with experiment after a uniform energy shift, accurately capturing spectral shapes and relative peak positions; only the rotatory strength of the lowest transition is underestimated.  
Analysis of the excited states reveals that the first band corresponds to a largely chromophore-centered $\pi\to\pi^\star$ transition with partial charge-transfer character.  
Importantly, the second absorption band originates from a $\pi\to\pi^\star$ transition localized on a tryptophan residue, while a positive ECD feature arises from a transition on a hydrogen-bonded tyrosine.  
These findings demonstrate that the full protein environment must be included to recover the correct spectral signatures, as chromophore-only models fail to describe such transitions.

	\centering

	\caption{UV/vis absorption and CD spectra of PYP in water in comparison with XsCAM-B3LYP/TDA/6-31+G(d)/631G//C-PCM(water) ones.}
	\label{fig:1pa+ecd-pyp}
\end{figure}

Together, these examples emphasize the complementary roles of ADQM and ASQM: the former enables a realistic treatment of complex chromophores in solution or flexible biomolecules by incorporating structural dynamics, whereas the latter delivers accurate spectra for comparatively rigid systems at a reduced computational cost, while preserving a fully quantum-mechanical description of the entire assembly.
