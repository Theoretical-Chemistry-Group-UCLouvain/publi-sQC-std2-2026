\section{Introduction}

The accurate theoretical characterization of electronic excited states and response properties in complex molecular environments remains a central challenge for computational chemistry.  
Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) is widely used for medium-sized systems, yet its formal $\mathcal O(N^4)$ scaling and known limitations (the treatment of charge-transfer, Rydberg, and doubly excited states) typically restrict routine applications to a few hundred atoms.
As researchers increasingly investigate biomolecular assemblies, supramolecular aggregates, and functional materials, they require methods that combine predictive accuracy with favorable computational scaling.\cite{burschBestPracticeDFTProtocols2022}


Simplified quantum chemistry (sQC) methods have emerged as an effective route to bridge the longstanding gap between predictive accuracy and computational feasibility.\cite{bannwarthExtendedTightbindingQuantum2021,loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
Their development was largely motivated by the cost of modern \textit{ab initio} wave-function approaches and TD-DFT, which, despite their improved reliability, remained restricted to relatively small systems.  
Prior to their widespread adoption, semiempirical techniques constituted the primary tools for modeling excited states in large molecules.\cite{thielSemiempiricalQuantumChemical2014,loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
These methodologies frequently relied on the zero-differential overlap (ZDO) approximation.
Among these, the ZINDO/S method\cite{ridleyIntermediateNeglectDifferential1973,zernerSemiempiricalMolecularOrbital1991} (Intermediate Neglect of Differential Overlap using Configuration Interaction with Singles) proved particularly successful for describing vertical excitations and electronic spectra, enabling early studies of complex chromophores and biological assemblies.  
Response functions, such as the first hyperpolarizability, were also considered early on.\cite{papadopoulosCalculationsInducedMoments1982,waiteCalculationsInducedMoments1982,nicolaidesCalculationsInducedMoments1982}
Later on, methods based on the Neglect of Diatomic Differential Overlap\cite{dewarGroundStatesMolecules1977,dewarDevelopmentUseQuantum1985} (NDDO) with orthogonalization corrections, including the OM$x$ families,\cite{kolbMNDOModelMethodical1993} further improved the exploration of potential energy surfaces and excited-state dynamics.  

The modern simplified framework was inaugurated with the introduction of the simplified Tamm--Dancoff approximation (sTDA) in 2013 by Grimme,\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013} followed shortly by its TD-DFT counterpart (sTD-DFT).\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
These methods achieve substantial acceleration through controlled approximations, including the neglect of the exchange--correlation kernel and approximate expression for two-electron integrals (see below).  
This approach was subsequently extended to ultra-large systems through the sTD-DFT-xTB variant,\cite{grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016} enabling explicit quantum-mechanical excited-state calculations for systems containing several thousand atoms, thanks to an early implementation of the xTB scheme.  
Alternative schemes inspired by the success of sTD-DFT/sTDA have then been proposed to further accelerate excited-state calculations.
The TD-DFT+TB method introduced by Rüger \emph{et al.}\cite{rugerTightbindingApproximationsTimedependent2016} combines a DFT ground state with a monopole approximation and local exchange--correlation functionals, delivering speedups of up to two orders of magnitude while maintaining errors below $\sim$0.15~eV for excitation energies in large plasmonic systems.
Giannone and Della Sala recently proposed the TD-DFT-as approach,\cite{giannoneMinimalAuxiliaryBasis2020} based on a resolution-of-identity scheme with a single $s$-type auxiliary function per atom, from which sTD-DFT, sTDA, and TD-DFT+TB can be viewed as approximations; this strategy was subsequently extended to hybrid functionals through TD-DFT-ris.\cite{zhouMinimalAuxiliaryBasis2023}
Additional developments include the extension of TD-DFT+TB to analytical excited-state gradients, the incorporation of periodic boundary conditions within sTDA,\cite{hehnExcitedStatePropertiesExtended2022} and the introduction of simplified GW/BSE approaches,\cite{choSimplifiedGWBSE2022} collectively illustrating the sustained effort to reduce computational cost while preserving predictive accuracy.
As a consequence, both sTDA and TD-DFT-ris can serve as preconditionner for actual full TD-DFT calculation, resulting in non-negligible speep-ups.\cite{zhouAcceleratingMolecularProperty2021,zhouConvergingTimeDependentDensity2024}

Over time, the sTD framework has expanded to encompass a broad spectrum of linear and nonlinear response properties,\cite{dewergifossePerspectiveSimplifiedQuantum2021} such as polarizabilities, hyperpolarizabilities,\cite{dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2018} two-photon absor\-ption,\cite{dewergifosseUltrafastEvaluationTwoPhoton2022} excited-state absorption,\cite{dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2019} optical rotation,\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2020} and spin-flip formulations for strongly correlated situations,\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedSpinFlipTimeDependent2019}  thereby transforming simplified methods into versatile tools for large-scale simulations.  
Recent developments have led to the eXact-integral simplified TD-DFT (XsTD-DFT) methodology,\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} which moves the simplified framework toward a more rigorous first-principles formulation.  
By replacing semiempirical integral approximations with exact one- and two-center atomic-orbital integrals, XsTD-DFT removes the dependence on fitted parameters while preserving favorable scaling.  
The extension to support range-separated hybrid functionals\cite{dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} further improves the description for long-range charge-transfer excitations and nonlinear optical responses through the solution of the full Casida equations.  
Taken together, these advances establish simplified TD-DFT as a reliable platform for predictive simulations of very large molecular systems.  

These capabilities have been implemented in the open-source \texttt{std2} program\cite{std2zenodo} (formerly distributed as \texttt{stda}), which provides a modular environment for computing excited states together with a broad range of linear and nonlinear response properties, including one- (1PA), electronic circular dichroism (ECD), and two-photon absorption (2PA) spectra, optical rotation (OR), second-harmonic generation (SHG), and excited-state absorption (ESA).  
In this article, we present an overview of the capabilities of the program \texttt{std2}, beginning with a concise description of the underlying theoretical framework in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, followed by benchmark assessments and representative applications that illustrate its performance across diverse spectroscopic observables and system sizes.  

The availability of robust and computationally efficient methodologies now makes it possible to explicitly target: \begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item large conformational ensembles, and/or  
	\item large systems and realistic molecular environments at full quantum-mechanical level.\cite{loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
\end{inparaenum}
Indeed, while continuum solvation models, such as PCM, neglect specific solute--environment interactions, multiscale strategies like QM/MM introduce dependencies on force fields, boundary definitions, and polarization treatments.  
All-atom quantum mechanical (AQM) strategies\cite{loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024} therefore provide an appealing alternative by treating the entire system at the QM level, avoiding locality assumptions while naturally capturing some long-range effects.  
Within this framework, two complementary paradigms have emerged (Fig.~\ref{fig:aqm}): the All-Atom Single-structure QM (ASQM) and the All-Atom Dynamic QM (ADQM) approaches. \cite{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}
ASQM evaluates response properties for a representative structure and is particularly effective for rigid systems whose optical signatures are only weakly affected by structural fluctuations.  
By contrast, ADQM incorporates conformational sampling, typically through molecular dynamics (MD) trajectories or Boltzmann-weighted ensembles, thereby accounting for thermal disorder and environment-induced spectral broadening.  
Together, ASQM and ADQM define a unified strategy for predictive spectroscopy of large molecular systems, enabling the level of structural realism to be matched to the physical complexity of the problem; these approaches constitute one of the focus of Section~\ref{sec:illu}.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

In this section, the (X)sTD-DFT theory will be explained. Unless otherwise mentioned, $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$,... refer to molecular orbitals (MOs), $i$, $j$,... to occupied, $a$, $b$,... to unoccupied ones, $\ket{m}$,... to electronic excited states, and $\mu$, $\nu$,... to atomic orbitals (AOs). 
Atomic units should be assumed throughout.

\subsection{Basics of TD-DFT}

The solution of the electronic Schrödinger equation constitutes the foundation of \emph{ab initio} quantum chemistry.  
Within density functional theory (DFT), this problem is recast in terms of the one-electron density, $\rho(\vec{r})$, rather than the many-electron wavefunction.  
In practice, one is often interested in molecular properties associated with measurable physical observables.  
When these cannot be expressed as expectation values of suitable operators, they are defined as response properties of the system to external perturbations, within the framework of perturbation theory.  
Typical examples include:  
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item Nuclear displacements ($\Delta \vec{x}$), which give rise to molecular gradients (first order) and harmonic vibrational frequencies derived from the Hessian (second order);
	\item Time-dependent magnetic fields $\vec{B}(t)$, which lead to properties such as NMR shieldings;
	\item Time-dependent electric fields $\vec{F}(t)$, which provide access to the polarizability ($\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$), the first hyperpolarizability ($\dstack{\beta}{\dstack{\leftrightarrow}{\rightarrow}}$), and higher-order responses; and
	\item Mixed properties, such as optical rotation, via the electric--magnetic polarizability tensor, $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$.
\end{inparaenum}
In this context, the induced dipole moment in the presence of an electromagnetic field can be expressed as a Taylor expansion,
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\mu}_{\mathrm{ind}} = \dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}\cdot\vec{F}
	+ \frac{1}{2}\dstack{\beta}{\dstack{\leftrightarrow}{\rightarrow}}:\vec{F}\vec{F}
	+ \dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}
	+ \ldots
\end{equation}
However, the Hohenberg-Kohn and Kohn-Sham theorems are valid only for stationary (time-independent) situations. 
Fortunately, Runge and Gross~\cite{rungeDensityFunctionalTheoryTimeDependent1984} extended DFT to the time-dependent domain by proving that the time-dependent electron density uniquely determines the time-dependent external potential, thereby generalizing the first Hohenberg-Kohn theorem. 
Furthermore, van Leeuwen~\cite{vanleeuwenCausalitySymmetryTimeDependent1998} demonstrated that Kohn-Sham orbitals satisfy the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. 
The adiabatic approximation is then commonly invoked, meaning that the time-dependent XC potential is approximated by evaluating the ground-state XC functional at the instantaneous density:
\begin{equation}
	\hat V_{XC}(\vec{r}, t) \approx 
	\frac{\partial E_{XC}[\rho]}{\partial \rho(\vec{r}, t)}. \label{eq:adiabatic}
\end{equation}

In practice, both linear and nonlinear response functions can thus be obtained analytically, and we refer to Refs. \citenum{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995} and \citenum{parkerQuadraticResponseProperties2018} for an in-depth introduction. 
In Density-based formalism of TD-DFT, for a time-dependent perturbation operator $\hat V$ oscillating at frequency $\omega$ and polarized in direction $\zeta$, and assuming real orbitals, the linear response vectors $\mathbf X(\omega)$ and $\mathbf Y(\omega)$ are obtained by solving:
\begin{equation}
	\left[
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\
		\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}
	\end{pmatrix}
	- \omega
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{0} \\
		\mathbf{0} & -\mathbf{1}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\right]
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X}_\zeta(\omega) \\
		\mathbf{Y}_\zeta(\omega)
	\end{pmatrix}
	=
	-\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{V}_\zeta \\
		\mathbf{V}_\zeta^\star
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
where the elements of the orbital gradient vectors, $\mathbf{V}_\zeta$ and $\mathbf{V}_\zeta^\star$, are given by ${V}_{\zeta, ia} = \langle i | \hat{V} | a \rangle$ and ${V}_{\zeta, ia}^\star = \langle a | \hat{V} | i \rangle$. 
These vectors collect the off-diagonal matrix elements of $\langle p | \hat{V} | q \rangle$ corresponding to excitation ($i \to a$) and de-excitation ($a \to i$) processes. 
The vectors $\mathbf{X}_\zeta(\omega)$ and $\mathbf{Y}_\zeta(\omega)$ therefore describe the excitation and de-excitation components of the linear response, respectively.
The matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ constitute the electronic Hessian (supermatrices). 
They are generally indexed using the occupied-virtual notation ($ia$, $jb$), indicating that each element involves one occupied and one unoccupied orbital. 
Assuming a global hybrid functional of the form $E_{XC} = (1-a_x)\,E_X^{\mathrm{GGA}} + a_x\,E_X^{\mathrm{HF}} + E_C^{\mathrm{GGA}}$, their elements, obtained via the Slater-Condon rules, are:
\begin{align}
	A_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 2\,(ia|jb)
	- a_x\,(ij|ab)
	+ (1-a_x)\,(ia|f_{XC}|jb),
	\nonumber \\
	B_{ia,jb} &= 2\,(ia|bj)
	- a_x\,(ib|aj)
	+ (1-a_x)\,(ia|f_{XC}|bj),
	\label{eq:TD-AandB}
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_i$ and $\varepsilon_a$ denote occupied and virtual orbital energies, respectively, and $a_x$ is the fraction of nonlocal Fock exchange. 
The terms $(ia|jb)$, $(ia|bj)$, and $(ib|aj)$ denote two-electron exchange-type integrals, while $(ij|ab)$ denotes a Coulomb-type integral. 
The terms $(ia|f_{XC}|jb)$ and $(ia|f_{XC}|bj)$ represent exchange-correlation kernel contributions (deriving from Eq.~\eqref{eq:adiabatic}). 
In the Tamm-Dancoff approximation (TDA),\cite{hirataTimedependentDensityFunctional1999} the $\mathbf{B}$ matrix is neglected.

Because the poles of the response function correspond to electronic excitation energies, it is common to set $\mathbf{V}_\zeta = 0$, yielding the non-hermitian eigenvalue problem:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\
		\mathbf{B} & \mathbf{A}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X} \\
		\mathbf{Y}
	\end{pmatrix}
	=
	\omega_m
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{0} \\
		\mathbf{0} & -\mathbf{1}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{X} \\
		\mathbf{Y}
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:casida}
\end{equation}
which is known as the Casida equation~\cite{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995}. 
More precisely, Eq.~\eqref{eq:casida} is a symplectic eigenvalue problem,\cite{kauczorEfficiencyAlgorithmsSolving2011,furcheAcceleratingMolecularProperty2016} where each positive eigenvalue $\omega_m$ corresponds to a transition energy from the ground state $\ket{0}$ to an excited state $\ket{m}$. 
The associated amplitude vectors $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ describe, respectively, the excitation and de-excitation components of each transition.

Once the excitation or response vectors are computed, they can be assembled into the desired property tensors (see below). 
In practice, this is done via the computation of the $\mathbf A \pm \mathbf B$ matrices, rather than the $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$ matrices themselves.\cite{casidaTimeDependentDensityFunctional1995,bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014,furcheAcceleratingMolecularProperty2016,parkerQuadraticResponseProperties2018}
For large systems, however, the dimensions of the $\mathbf{A} \pm \mathbf{B}$ matrices and the cost of computing their elements rapidly become prohibitive.
This is the topic of the next subsection.

\subsection{The simplified TD-DFT framework}

In essence, simplified TD-DFT methods achieve substantial reductions in computational cost through three key approximations, while maintaining sufficient accuracy for a wide range of applications: 
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item the excitation space is truncated by retaining only the most relevant configuration state functions (CSFs), based on selection using energy thresholds, $E_w$ (selecting an active space) and $E_{thr}$ (selecting the CSFs), followed by a perturbative treatment (see below),
	\item one-electron integrals are evaluated exactly, whereas two-electron integrals are approximated using the ZDO framework in the Löwdin space, with different treatments of the electron--repulsion integrals (ERIs) defining the various simplified TD-DFT variants, and
	\item all XCF contributions to the response equations are neglected.
\end{inparaenum}

As a consequence, each (X)sTD-DFT calculation proceeds through the following sequence of steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:computationalsequence}): 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The active molecular-orbital space is defined as
	$\varepsilon_p \in [\varepsilon_{\mathrm{LUMO}} - E'_w,\; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HOMO}} + E'_w]$,
	where $E'_w = 2\,(1 + 0.8\,a_x)\,E_w$.\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
	\item From this space, primary CSFs (P-CSFs) $\ket{\Psi_i^a}$ are selected according to the criterion $A'_{ia,ia} < E_{\mathrm{thr}}$.
	Configurations $\ket{\Psi_j^b}$ with $A'_{jb,jb} > E_{\mathrm{thr}}$ are considered as candidates for a secondary set (S-CSFs), and are retained if their perturbative contribution exceeds a threshold $E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{thr}}$.
	This contribution is defined as
	\begin{equation*}
		E^{(2)}_{jb} 
		= \sum_{ia \in \mathrm{P\text{-}CSFs}} 
		\frac{|A'_{ia,jb}|^2}{A'_{jb,jb} - A'_{ia,ia}},
	\end{equation*}
	which quantifies the cumulative second-order coupling of a given S-CSF $\ket{\Psi_j^b}$ with the selected P-CSFs.
	The approximate matrix elements (denoted with a a prime), $A'_{ia,jb}$, are evaluated using the approximate expressions introduced below.
	All remaining configurations are discarded.
	This procedure defines the so-called \emph{single-threshold} selection scheme.
	A \emph{dual-threshold} variant can also be employed, in which a larger threshold is applied to select CSFs that include atoms of interest (\textit{e.g.}, a chromophore).\cite{beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021,loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}
	\item Construct the reduced supermatrices $\mathbf{A'} \pm \mathbf{B'}$ (or only $\mathbf{A'}$ in the TDA), representing a subset of the full matrices, using the selected CSFs and the approximate expressions for their elements (see below).
	\item Solve for the excitation or response vectors [Eqs.~\eqref{eq:response} or \eqref{eq:casida}].
	\item Assemble the relevant property tensors, noting that XCF contributions remain neglected at this level.
\end{enumerate}

Two simplified TD-DFT variants are implemented in \text{std2}: the recently-introduced XsTD-DFT approach,\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} and the original sTD-DFT formalism.\cite{bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}  

\subsubsection{XsTD-DFT}

The XsTD-DFT method (generally abbreviated XsTD) was specifically designed to overcome several shortcomings of sTD-DFT, most notably its limited reliability for systems with substantial long-range exchange or pronounced charge-transfer character.  
XsTD-DFT assumes a range-separated (RSH) or global hybdrid (GH) XCFs,\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} and constructs the approximate TD-DFT supermatrix elements by removing all explicit XCF contributions. The resulting expressions are, for a RSH and a closed-shell system:
\begin{align}
	(\mathbf A'+ \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &=  \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 4\,(ia|jb)' - a_x\,[(ib|aj)'+(ij|ab)'] \nonumber\\&\hspace{2em}- c_x\,[(ij|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|ab)'+ (ib|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|aj)'],\nonumber\\
	(\mathbf A'-\mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)+  a_x\,[(ib|aj)'  - (ij|ab)'] \nonumber\\&\hspace{2em}-c_x\,[(ij|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|ab)'-(ib|\mathrm{erf}(\omega r)|aj)'] . 
	\label{eq:xstddft}
\end{align}
Here, $a_x$ and $c_x$ denote the constant and long-range fractions of Hartree--Fock exchange, respectively, and $\omega$ is the RSH range-separation parameter.
Note that unrestricted TD-DFT is also implemented for GH in the \texttt{std2} program to characterize singlet, doublet, or triplet states.

The approximate form of the $(ia|jb)'$ integrals follows from a sequence of controlled simplifications.  
We begin with the general expression of the two-electron integrals in the MO basis,
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) = \sum_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} C_{i\mu}^\star C_{a\alpha} C^\star_{j\nu} C_{b\beta}\,(\mu\alpha|\nu\beta),
\end{equation*}
where $(\mu\alpha|\nu\beta)$ denotes an electron repulsion integral (ERI).  
Introducing the Löwdin-orthogonalized coefficients $\mathbf{C}^\perp = \mathbf{S}^{1/2}\mathbf{C}$ yields
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) = \sum_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}( C_{i\mu}^\perp)^\star C_{a\alpha}^\perp (C^\perp_{j\nu})^\star C_{b\beta}^\perp\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\alpha|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\beta),
\end{equation*}
where each orthogonalized AO is written as $\lambda_\mu = \sum_\beta \phi_\beta\,S_{\mu\beta}^{-1/2}$, with $\{\phi_\beta\}$ the original atomic basis functions.  
The ZDO approximation employed in our simplified methods corresponds to the complete neglect of differential overlap (CNDO):
\begin{equation}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\alpha|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\beta) \approx \delta_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\nu\beta}\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu), \label{eq:cndo}
\end{equation}
which leads to
\begin{equation*}
	(ia|jb) \approx (ia|jb)' = \sum_{\mu\nu}( C_{i\mu}^\perp)^\star C_{a\mu}^\perp (C^\perp_{j\nu})^\star C_{b\nu}^\perp\,(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu),
\end{equation*}
introducing some computational saving [integrals are evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(N_{AO}^2)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(N_{AO}^4)$], and where
\begin{equation}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu)
	= \sum_{\alpha\beta\kappa\eta} (\alpha\beta|\kappa\eta)\,
	S^{-1/2}_{\mu\alpha}S^{-1/2}_{\mu\beta}S^{-1/2}_{\nu\kappa}S^{-1/2}_{\nu\eta}.
	\label{eq:loweri}
\end{equation}
To analyze the behavior of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loweri}, it is customary\cite{fischer-hjalmarsZeroDifferentialOverlap1966,grayJustifiabilityZDOApproximation1970,chandlerReexaminationJustificationNeglect1980} to introduce a power-series expansion of $\mathbf S^{-1/2}$.  
Assuming that $\Delta_{\mu\nu} = S_{\mu\nu} -\delta_{\mu\nu}$ (thus $\forall\mu: \Delta_{\mu\mu} = 0$), one obtains
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf S^{-1/2} = (\mathbf I + \bm\Delta)^{-1/2} = \mathbf I - \frac{1}{2}\bm\Delta + \frac{3}{8}\bm\Delta^2 + \mathcal{O}(\bm\Delta^3),
	\label{eq:pows1/2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf I$ denotes the identity matrix.  
This yields\begin{equation*}
	\lambda_\mu \approx \phi_{\mu} - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\neq\mu} \Delta_{\mu\alpha}\phi_\alpha + \frac{3}{8}\sum_{\alpha\beta\neq\mu}  \Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}\phi_\beta + \mathcal{O}(\bm\Delta^3),
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{align*}
	(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu)  = (\mu\mu|\nu\nu) &-\sum_{\alpha\neq\mu} \Delta_{\mu\alpha} (\alpha\mu|\nu\nu) -\sum_{\alpha\neq\nu} \Delta_{\nu\alpha} (\mu\mu|\alpha\nu)\\
	&+\frac{3}{4}\sum_{\alpha\beta\neq \mu} \Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\alpha\beta} (\beta\mu|\nu\nu)+\frac{3}{4}\sum_{\alpha\beta\neq \nu} \Delta_{\nu\alpha}\Delta_{\alpha\beta} (\mu\mu|\beta\nu) \\
	&+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha\beta\neq \mu} \Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\mu\beta} (\alpha\beta|\nu\nu)+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha\beta\neq \nu} \Delta_{\nu\alpha}\Delta_{\nu\beta} (\mu\mu|\alpha\beta) \\
	&+\sum_{\alpha\neq\mu,\beta\neq\nu} \Delta_{\mu\alpha}\Delta_{\nu\beta} (\alpha\mu|\beta\nu) + \mathcal{O}(\bm\Delta^3).
\end{align*}
Since $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}<1$, in the present work we retain only the leading contribution, $(\lambda_\mu\lambda_\mu|\lambda_\nu\lambda_\nu) \approx (\mu\mu|\nu\nu)$.  
Higher-order terms could in principle be included in future developments; however, the 0\textsuperscript{th}-order approximation already provides an excellent estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loweri} in practice (see Figs.~S1--S3).  
Furthermore, the series in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pows1/2} is not guaranteed to converge, although its behavior can be rationalized in specific cases.\cite{chandlerReexaminationJustificationNeglect1980}  
Within the CNDO framework [Eq.~\eqref{eq:cndo}], the present truncation therefore constitutes a reasonable approximation.

Introducing the transition charges $q^{ia}_\mu = \big(C^{\perp}_{i\mu}\big)^\star C^{\perp}_{a\mu}$ finally yields
\begin{equation}
	(ia|jb)' = \sum_{\mu\nu} q^{ia}_\mu\,q^{jb}_\nu\,(\mu\mu|\nu\nu).
	\label{eq:xsints}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{sTD-DFT}

In contrast, the sTD-DFT model (generally written sTD) retains the GH form of the XCF (Eq.~\eqref{eq:TD-AandB}).  
The approximate electronic Hessian elements take the form\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013,bannwarthSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2014}
\begin{align}
	(\mathbf A'+ \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i)
	+ 4\,(ia|jb)'_K - a_x\,[(ib|aj)'_K+(ij|ab)'_J], \nonumber\\
	(\mathbf A' - \mathbf B')_{ia,jb} &= \delta_{ij}\delta_{ab} (\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_i) + a_x\,[(ib|aj)'_K-(ij|ab)'_J ].
	\label{eq:stddft}
\end{align}
In contrary to Eq.~\eqref{eq:xsints}, these integrals are evaluated semi-empirically through a atomic monopole-based representation, with the transition charges projected on atoms:
\begin{equation}
	(ia|jb)'_X = \sum_{AB} q_A^{ia}\,q_B^{jb}\,(AA|BB)_X, 
	\qquad 
	q_A^{ia} = \sum_{\mu \in A} \big(C^{\perp}_{i\mu}\big)^\star C^{\perp}_{a\mu},
	\label{eq:sints}
\end{equation}
where the sums now run over atomic centers $A$ and $B$.  
The required two-center terms $(AA|BB)_X$ are evaluated with Mataga--Nishimoto--Ohno--Klopman (MNOK) damped Coulomb operators.\cite{klopmanSemiempiricalTreatmentMolecular1964}  
For Coulomb-type contributions ($X=J$),
\begin{equation*}
	a_x\,(AA|BB)_J =
	\left[\frac{1}{R_{AB}^{\gamma_J} + \left(a_x\,\eta_{AB}\right)^{-\gamma_J}}\right]^{1/\gamma_J},
\end{equation*}
where $R_{AB}$ is the interatomic distance, $\eta_{AB} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_A + \eta_B)$ is the average chemical hardness, and $\gamma_J$ is a global damping parameter.  
Note that this formulation is choosen so that for $R_{AB}\to 0$, the integral simplifies to $a_x\,\eta$ which is consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:TD-AandB}.\cite{grimmeSimplifiedTammDancoffDensity2013}
Exchange-type integrals ($X=K$) employ
\begin{equation*}
	(AA|BB)_K =
	\left[\frac{1}{R_{AB}^{\gamma_K} + \eta_{AB}^{-\gamma_K}}\right]^{1/\gamma_K}.
\end{equation*}



\subsection{Obtaining and interpreting responses}

Initially developed for UV/Vis and ECD spectroscopies, the simplified TD-DFT framework has since been extended to a broad range of linear and quadratic molecular response properties, and  their residues.\cite{dewergifossePerspectiveSimplifiedQuantum2021,loffelsenderSimplifiedQuantumChemistry2024}
Formally, any linear response function admits a sum-over-states representation:\cite{orrPerturbationTheoryNonlinear1971}
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A; \hat B}}_{\omega} = \sum_{\ket{m}} \frac{\braket{0|\hat A|m}\braket{m|\hat B|0}}{\omega-\omega_m} - \frac{\braket{0|\hat B|m}\braket{m|\hat A|0}}{\omega+\omega_m}.
	\label{eq:soslin}
\end{equation}
For $\hat A = \hat B = \hat\mu$ ($\hat\mu$ is the electric dipole operator), Eq.~\eqref{eq:soslin} yields the electric dipole polarizability $\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$, whereas choosing $\hat A = \hat\mu$ and $\hat B = \hat m$ ($\hat m$ is the magnetic moment operator) gives access to the electric--magnetic dipole polarizability $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$.
Using the response vectors defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:response}, the same quantity can be recast as
\begin{equation}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = Tr[A_\zeta \gamma^{(\pm)}_\eta(\omega)] = 2\,\sum_{ia} A_{\zeta,ia}\,\underbrace{[X_{\eta, ia}(\omega) \pm Y_{\eta,ia}(\omega)]}_{\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta,ia}(\omega)}, 
	\label{eq:linresp}
\end{equation}
where the occupied--virtual elements of $\mathbf A_\zeta$ are given by $\braket{i|\hat A_\zeta|a}$, and $\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega)$ defines the frequency-dependent transition density matrix ($\omega$PDM), and the factor 2 is introduced assuming a closed-shell wavefunction.  
For real operators $\hat A$ such as $\hat\mu$, the $+$ branch is used to obtain the relevant tensor components of $\dstack{\alpha}{\leftrightarrow}$ and $\dstack{G}{\leftrightarrow}$.

Residues of Eq.~\eqref{eq:soslin} yield the ground-to-excited-state transition moments,
\begin{equation*}
	\lim_{\omega\to\omega_m} (\omega-\omega_m)\braket{\braket{\hat A; \hat B}}_{\omega} = \braket{0|\hat A|m}\braket{m|\hat B|0},
\end{equation*}
which can be evaluated directly from the amplitude vectors:
\begin{equation}
	A_{\zeta}^{0m} = \braket{0|A_\zeta|m} = \sqrt{2}\,\sum_{ia} \mu_{\zeta,ia}\underbrace{[X_{ia} \pm Y_{ia}]}_{\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m,ia}}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m}$ corresponds to the one-electron transition density matrix (1TDM), with $\gamma^{(+)}_{0m}$ used for UV/Vis intensities ($\vec\mu_{0m}$) and $\gamma^{(-)}_{0m}$ for ECD rotational strengths ($\vec m_{0m}$).

Analogous expressions provide access to quadratic response properties and their associated residues.\cite{dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2018,dewergifosseNonlinearresponsePropertiesSimplified2019,dewergifosseUltrafastEvaluationTwoPhoton2022}
In deriving these expressions, the exchange--correlation hyperkernel ($g_{XC}$) is neglected, such that the resulting formalism corresponds to the ``unrelaxed'' approximation introduced by Furche and co-workers.\cite{parkerQuadraticResponseProperties2018}
At present, the implementation includes first hyperpolarizabilities ($\beta$), governing SHG, as well as its residues: 2PA and ESA.

To enable chemically transparent interpretation, both the linear response and its residues may be expressed in terms of natural response orbitals (NROs)\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} or, in the resonant limit, natural transition orbitals (NTOs).\cite{dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019}  
Within the NTO framework, the 1TDM admits a compact singular-value decomposition,
\begin{equation}
	\gamma^{(\pm)}_{0m} = \mathbf U\Lambda_{0m}\mathbf V^T, \label{eq:ntos}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf U$ and $\mathbf V$ define the hole and particle orbital subspaces, respectively, and $\Lambda_{0m} = \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\sqrt{\lambda_2},\ldots)$.  
The set $\{\lambda_i\}$ provides the weight of each NTO, and the effective number of contributing pairs is quantified via the participation ratio:
\begin{equation*}
	PR_{NTO} = \frac{[\sum_i\lambda_i]^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2}.
\end{equation*}
Typically, only a small number of NTO pairs dominate, providing a chemically intuitive picture of the excitation.

Analogously, the frequency-dependent $\omega$PDM can be compressed into NROs\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} via
\begin{equation}
	\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega) = \mathbf U\,\Lambda_\eta\,\mathbf V^T,\label{eq:nros}
\end{equation}
leading to
\begin{equation*}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = 2 \sum_i \Upsilon_{\zeta,i}\Lambda_{ \eta,i}(\omega) = \sum_i R_{\zeta\eta, i}(\omega),
\end{equation*}
with $\Upsilon_\zeta = \mathbf U^T \mathbf A_\zeta \mathbf V$.  
The weight of each NRO contribution is given by
\begin{equation*}
	w_i(\omega) = \frac{|R_{\zeta\eta,i}(\omega)|}{\sum_i R_{\zeta\eta, i}(\omega)},
\end{equation*}
and the corresponding participation ratio reads
\begin{equation*}
	PR_{NRO}(\omega)  = \frac{[\sum_i w_i(\omega)]^2}{\sum_i w_i(\omega)}.
\end{equation*}
An alternative perspective is obtained by applying a Löwdin orthogonalization to $\mathbf A$ and the 1TDM.  
Starting from $\mathbf C = \mathbf P \Sigma \mathbf Q^T$, one defines
\begin{equation*}
	\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\zeta} = (\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T) \mathbf A_\zeta(\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)^T
	\quad\text{and}\quad
	\tilde{\Lambda}_{\eta}(\omega) = (\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)[\gamma^{(\pm)}_{\eta}(\omega)](\mathbf P\mathbf Q^T)^T,
\end{equation*}
such that
\begin{equation*}
	\braket{\braket{\hat A_\zeta; \hat B_\eta}}_{\omega} = 2 \sum_{\mu\nu} \tilde\Upsilon_{\zeta,\mu\nu}\tilde\Lambda_{ \eta,\mu\nu}(\omega) = \sum_{\mu\nu} \tilde R_{\zeta\eta, \mu\nu}(\omega).
\end{equation*}
This formulation decomposes the response into atomic-orbital contributions, which may be further aggregated over chemically meaningful fragments.  
The resulting site (AA) and inter-fragment (AB) terms are given by
\begin{equation*}
	\Omega_{\zeta\eta}^{AB}(\omega) = 2\sum_{\mu\in A,\nu\in B} \tilde R_{\zeta\eta, \mu\nu}(\omega).
\end{equation*}
Together, these two complementary decompositions constitute the RespA protocol,\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020} enabling concise and chemically interpretable descriptions of both static and frequency-dependent response phenomena.

\subsection{Spin-flip variant}

The spin-flip (SF) method provides an efficient framework for the description of strongly correlated systems, such as diradicals, which are often challenging for standard single-reference approaches.  
Proposed by Krylov and co-workers,\cite{krylovSizeconsistentWaveFunctions2001} it relies on a well-defined high-spin reference state, typically a triplet, and accesses target low-spin states through spin-flipping excitations with $\Delta S_z = -1$.  
This formulation enables the inclusion of one configuration that correspond to  a double excitation with respect to a closed-shell reference, thereby capturing static correlation effects at a modest computational cost.  
Combined with simplified approaches, SF-sTD-DFT offers a practical route for treating large strongly correlated systems.\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedSpinFlipTimeDependent2019}  
In contrast to conventional TD-DFT, the method describes open-shell singlet states through $\alpha \rightarrow \beta$ spin-flip excitations starting from the high-spin reference.\cite{dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019}

Within the Tamm--Dancoff approximation (TDA), the eigenvalue problem reads
\begin{equation*}
	\mathbf{A}^{\alpha \rightarrow \beta} \mathbf{X} = \omega_{SF} \mathbf{X},
\end{equation*}
where $\omega_{SF}$ denotes the excitation energy and $\mathbf{X}$ the corresponding eigenvector.  
The matrix elements of $\mathbf{A}^{\alpha \rightarrow \beta}$ are given by
\begin{equation*}
	A^{\alpha\to\beta}_{i_\alpha a_\beta, j_\alpha b_\beta} =  \delta_{i_\alpha j_\alpha}\delta_{a_\beta b_\beta} (\varepsilon_{a_\beta} - \varepsilon_{i_\alpha}) - a_x\,(i_\alpha j_\alpha|a_\beta b_\beta)',
\end{equation*}
where the two-electron integral $(i_\alpha j_\alpha|a_\beta b_\beta)'$ is approximated within the sTD-DFT framework using Eq.~\eqref{eq:sints}, with a modified damped Coulomb operator
\begin{equation*}
	a_x\,(AA|BB)_J =
	\left[\frac{1}{R_{AB}^{\gamma_{\alpha\to\beta}} + \left(1.4\,a_x\,\eta_{AB}\right)^{-\gamma_{\alpha\to\beta}}}\right]^{1/\gamma_{\alpha\to\beta}},
\end{equation*}
where $\gamma_{\alpha\to\beta} = a_x + 0.3$.  
Note that the framework was recently extended using the improvements described above in order to provide the SF-XsTD-DFT scheme.

While SF is first a ground state method, absorption intensities are obtained from state-to-state transition dipole moments derived from the double residue of the quadratic response function.  
In practice, a factor of $\sqrt 2$ is applied to these transition moments to approximately compensate for the missing excitations in the $\alpha$ space, improving agreement with experimental intensities for diradical systems.  
Overall, this framework enables the computation of electronic spectra for systems containing thousands of atoms at a fraction of the cost of conventional SF-TD-DFT, while remaining fully compatible with NTO-based analyses.\cite{dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019}



\subsection{Implementation details}

The methods discussed in the previous sections are implemented in the \texttt{std2} program (currently version~2.0.1\cite{std2zenodo}), freely available on GitHub (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/std2}). 
The program is written in Fortran and uses the \texttt{libcint}\cite{libcint} library for the evaluation of atomic-orbital integrals, while linear-algebra routines are delegated to either netlib's BLAS/LAPACK or Intel's oneAPI MKL, to be chosen at compilation times.
Its structures follows closely the one of Fig.~\ref{fig:computationalsequence}.
The most computationally demanding modules, which are the CSFs screening, the $\mathbf A'\pm \mathbf B'$ supermatrices construction, and the formation of the resulting tensors, are parallelized using OpenMP.
The code is distributed with limited user support, provided trough GitHub issues. 
It is built and installed using either Make or Meson, with an automatic adaptation to supported platforms. 
\texttt{std2} also distributed with a limited test suite. 

The executable works as a command-line input (CLI) program, whose options are documented in the GitHub repository (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/std2/blob/master/std2_manual.pdf}).


Every (X)sTD-DFT calculation requires a ground-state wavefunction as input. 
For this purpose, \texttt{std2} reads MOLDEN-formatted files, which are produced by most quantum-chemistry packages.
If needed, external tools such as Multiwfn\cite{luMultiwfnMultifunctionalWavefunction2012} can also convert wavefunction information into MOLDEN format.
For very large systems, one of the main application domains of \texttt{std2},\cite{beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021,lescosSelfassemblingStructureNonlinear2021,loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025} obtaining a sufficiently accurate ground-state wavefunction at a standard DFT level may be challenging or prohibitively costly. 
To address this, the auxiliary program \texttt{xtb4stda} (\url{https://github.com/grimme-lab/xtb4stda}) has been developed.\cite{grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016}
It generates a wavefunction at the xTB level using a specially optimized augmented basis set that includes an additional set of polarization functions, significantly improving the description of the virtual orbital space, which is traditionally a weakness of GFN$x$-xTB methods. 
We expect the upcoming g-xTB approach\cite{froitzheimGxTBGeneralPurposeExtended2025} explicitly designed to remedy deficiencies in the HOMO-LUMO gap and virtual space to further enhance the quality of such starting wavefunctions.

\section{Illustrations}\label{sec:illu}

In this section, recent examples of calculations will be used to demonstrate the capabilities and performances of the \texttt{std2} program. 
Throughout the text, the shorthand ``sXCF'' and ``XsXCF'' (e.g., sB3LYP) will be used to indicate that, starting from a ground-state calculation performed with said XCF, the TD-DFT part have been performed using the sTD or XsTD framework.


\subsection{Electronic excitations: 1PA and ECD spectra}


Historically, the use of \texttt{std2} (formerly \texttt{stda}) has progressively expanded from early applications in optical spectroscopy to the routine modeling of UV/Vis and ECD spectra for increasingly complex systems, reflecting its broad adoption by the quantum chemistry community. 
Notably, many of these usages were carried out independently of the original method developers, underscoring the transferability and practical appeal of the approach. 
Early studies established the feasibility of simplified TD-DFT methods for describing electronic spectra and photophysical properties of medium-sized systems, including photochromic molecules and coordination compounds, with substantial computational savings compared to conventional TD-DFT.\cite{wiebelerOpticalSpectrumPerceived2014,bannwarthElectronicCircularDichroism2015,chmelaDifferentialManyBodyCooperativity2016,sorgBismuthVersatileCation2018} 
Shortly thereafter, sTD-DFT was successfully applied to the interpretation of chiroptical properties, in particular ECD spectra used for absolute configuration assignments in organic and bioorganic molecules.\cite{bannwarthElectronicCircularDichroism2016,gehroldAxialHelicalPlanar2016,covingtonAtropoisomerismBiflavonesAbsolute2016} 
Systematic assessments further demonstrated the reliability of these simplified approaches for UV/Vis spectroscopy across families of chromophores, highlighting their ability to reproduce spectral trends at a fraction of the computational cost, especially when combined with hybrid or range-separated functionals.\cite{martynovMethodologicalSurveySimplified2019} 
The introduction of xTB-based variants\cite{grimmeUltrafastComputationElectronic2016} significantly extended the applicability to very large systems, enabling the treatment of biomacromolecules\cite{liuUltraFastComputationExcitedStates2021,beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021} and complex aggregates, such as dye assemblies where configurational sampling is essential.\cite{usoltsevSemiEmpiricalCalculationBodipy2022} 
More recently, sTDA/sTD-DFT has been integrated into high-throughput and data-driven workflows, where machine-learning calibration schemes mitigate systematic errors and enable large-scale screening of excited-state properties with near-TD-DFT accuracy.\cite{vermaMachineLearnedCalibrations2022} 
In parallel, developments targeting extended and periodic systems have demonstrated that simplified approaches retain a favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, thereby enabling applications to nanostructures and functional materials.\cite{hehnExcitedStatePropertiesExtended2022,nguyenTheoreticalInvestigationElectronic2022}
More recently, the applicability of the sTD-DFT framework was further extended to metallic nanoclusters, highlighting its potential for the description of plasmonic and nanoscale excited-state phenomena.\cite{Kebabsa2026sTDNanoClusters}


As mentioned in the previous section, the recently introduced XsTD formalism further improves accuracy by restoring key integral contributions at limited additional cost.\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024} 
Establishing the reliability of these low-cost approaches is therefore a prerequisite before extending their use to realistic, large-scale systems.
Reference~\citenum{maraldiEvaluatingPerformanceExact2025} therefore evaluates the performance of TD, sTD, and XsTD methods for 1PA spectra using several hybrid and range-separated hybrid exchange--correlation functionals, benchmarking against RI-CC2 results obtained with the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set for the first bright excitation ($f_{0\to n} > 0.02$). 
Results for the CT (QUEST) dataset are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-ct} (Fig.~S4). 
These datasets comprise small- to medium-sized push--pull $\pi$-conjugated molecules dominated by valence excitations, often with pronounced charge-transfer character. 
XsTD-DFT closely tracks conventional TD-DFT for both excitation energies ($\Delta E_{0\to n}$) and oscillator strengths ($f_{0\to n}$), as reflected by comparable mean deviations (MDs) and mean absolute deviations (MADs). 
In particular, XsCAM-B3LYP shows excellent agreement with RI-CC2 across both datasets. 
By contrast, sTD-DFT exhibits larger errors, especially with functionals containing high fractions of Hartree--Fock exchange (e.g., BHHLYP and M06-2X). 
Overall, the XsTD method emerges as a robust low-cost alternative to TD-DFT, reducing CPU time by roughly two to three orders of magnitude while maintaining similar accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-timing}).

The XsTDA approximation, which provides additional computational savings, likewise reproduces TDA-DFT trends with good fidelity.\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024,dewergifosseComputingExcitedStates2024}
Relative to their non-simplified counterparts, MADs of 0.14 and \SI{0.22}{\electronvolt} are obtained for XsTDA/B3LYP and XsTDA/CAM-B3LYP, respectively, compared to 0.16 and \SI{0.27}{\electronvolt} for the corresponding sTDA variants.

The capability of simplified approaches to describe the linear absorption of strongly correlated systems can be illustrated by large polycyclic diradicals such as teranthene and superoctazethrene derivatives (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-sf}).\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedSpinFlipTimeDependent2019} 
For these systems, SF formulations are required to properly account for the multiconfigurational character of the ground and low-lying excited states.  
Figure~\ref{fig:1pa-sf} compares experimental spectra to SF-TD-DFT, SF-sTD-DFT, and SF-XsTD-DFT results obtained with the B5050LYP functional.  
For the teranthene derivative, the experimental spectrum is dominated by a low-energy band at \SI{1.42}{\electronvolt}.  
This feature is reproduced most accurately by the SF-sTD-DFT method (\SI{1.48}{\electronvolt}), followed by SF-XsTD-DFT (\SI{1.53}{\electronvolt}), whereas the full SF-TD-DFT calculation predicts a more pronounced blue shift (\SI{1.66}{\electronvolt}).  
A similar trend is observed for superoctazethrene, for which the SF-sTD-DFT approach again provides the best agreement with the experimental absorption maximum.  
In this case, the full SF-TD-DFT scheme significantly blue-shifts the main band, although it reproduces the oscillator strength more accurately, while the new SF-XsTD-DFT result lies between both approaches.  
These results highlight the favorable balance achieved by the simplified formulation, which benefits from modified response integrals that tend to produce slightly red-shifted excitation energies relative to the full method, thereby improving agreement with respect to experiment for this class of diradicals.  
At the same time, the computational cost is drastically reduced, enabling the calculation of hundreds of spin-flip excited states within minutes on standard desktop hardware.  
For spin-flip calculations, however, the XsTD formulation provides only marginal improvements over the original simplified scheme.

The SF-sTD-DFT framework has also proven valuable for studying large polycyclic hydrocarbons with diradical character. 
For instance, investigations of indeno[1,2b]fluorene and related diindeno[n]thiophene derivatives\cite{dewergifosseAreFullyConjugated2019} showed that the method efficiently reproduces experimental absorption trends while allowing an intuitive interpretation of excited states using NTOs.  
Importantly, the spin-flip formulation captures small contributions from one doubly excited configuration that is inaccessible to conventional TD-DFT, leading to a consistent correlation between theoretical and experimental excitation energies.  
Beyond spectroscopy, SF-sTD-DFT has also been applied to the design of singlet-fission chromophores.\cite{kostadinovaSingletFissionTinkertoys2022,stoychevaSingletFissionTinkertoys}  
Because many efficient singlet-fission materials exhibit significant diradical character, the method provides a computationally efficient strategy to explore large candidate systems and rationalize how structural factors such as topology, conjugation length, or substituents influence singlet-fission propensity.

For chlorophyll~A (top panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:nexafs}), the carbon K-edge spectrum computed at the XsSRC2/TDA/cc-pCVDZ level reproduces the experimental lineshape with remarkable fidelity, despite a small systematic energy shift.

The scalability of the method is further illustrated by the PPG$_{10}$ collagen model  (bottom panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:nexafs}) comprising 600 atoms.  
Its oxygen K-edge spectrum, constructed from more than 85\,000 excited states, shows excellent agreement with respect to experiment across a broad energy window.  
The first absorption band is accurately reproduced in both position and width, with the most intense transition lying within \SI{0.6}{\electronvolt} of the experimental maximum and corresponding to a localized $1s_{\mathrm O}\rightarrow n/\pi^\star$ excitation on the peptide bond.  
Higher-energy features are likewise captured once appropriate spectral broadening is applied, reflecting the dense manifold of short-lived states above the ionization threshold.

The demonstrated accuracy and efficiency of sQC approaches make it possible to address a longstanding challenge in computational spectroscopy: the realistic simulation of molecules embedded in complex environments. 
Beyond the electronic structure method itself, reliable spectra require accounting for environmental effects, structural dynamics, and, when necessary, vibronic contributions. 
Flavin mononucleotide (FMN), discussed in Ref.~\citenum{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}, provides a stringent test case for these methodologies (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-fmn}). 
Gas-phase RI-CC2 and sTD-DFT-xTB spectra are remarkably similar and correctly reproduce the first experimental absorption band, validating the use of sTD-DFT-xTB as a reliable low-cost approach. 
Both methods, however, strongly blue-shift the second band, underscoring the decisive role of the aqueous environment. 
The top panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-fmn} compares spectra obtained with implicit solvent models within the ASQM framework and with a Boltzmann-weighted ensemble of structures (ADQM-Boltz.). 
While the inclusion of implicit solvation improves the first excitation energy, both approaches fail to reproduce the relative intensities and overall spectral shape. 
Moreover, Boltzmann averaging induces only minor changes compared to a single optimized structure, indicating that conformational diversity alone is insufficient to capture the experimental spectrum.
In contrast, the ADQM-MD protocol (bottom panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-fmn}), which explicitly includes solvent molecules and dynamical fluctuations through snapshots extracted from GFN2-xTB molecular dynamics simulations, yields striking agreement with respect to experiment without empirical energy shifts. 
Once a sufficiently large solvation sphere is considered, the positions, intensities, and broadening of the main absorption bands are accurately reproduced. 
These results demonstrate that the FMN spectrum is strongly governed by specific solute--solvent interactions and dynamical effects that cannot be captured by static or implicit solvent models.

The ADQM protocol is also essential for describing the electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra of flexible biomolecules, as it explicitly accounts for structural fluctuations.  
This is illustrated by myoglobin in Ref.~\citenum{seibertBiomolecularStructureInformation2017}, a prototypical member of the globin family responsible for intramuscular oxygen transport and comprising roughly 2500 atoms, well beyond the reach of conventional excited-state quantum-chemical methods.  
Within the ADQM-MD framework, performed at the sTDA-xTB level, the CD spectrum was obtained by averaging over 100 snapshots extracted from a molecular dynamics trajectory.  
This ensemble averaging suppresses artificial spectral features associated with individual conformations and yields a physically meaningful lineshape.  
The resulting spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:ecd-myo}) reproduces all characteristic experimental signatures with very good agreement.  
Because myoglobin contains eight $\alpha$-helices, the CD response is dominated by backbone transitions typical of helical peptides, confirming that the method correctly captures secondary-structure fingerprints while simultaneously accounting for the heme cofactor and its coupling to the protein environment.

Analysis of the excited states (Fig.~\ref{fig:nto-pyp}) via NTOs [Eq.~\eqref{eq:ntos}] provides further insight into the origin of the spectral features.  
The transition to the first excited state, which appears in both the UV/vis absorption and ECD spectra, corresponds to a $\pi \rightarrow \pi^\star$ excitation localized on the chromophore with partial charge-transfer (CT) character.  
The second main peak in the UV/vis absorption spectrum arises from a transition to the 23rd excited state, which also corresponds to a $\pi \to \pi^\star$ excitation but localized on a tryptophan residue of the protein.  
This finding directly explains why calculations performed on the isolated chromophore fail to reproduce this spectral feature.  
Only an all-atom quantum-mechanical treatment, such as the XsTDA approach, can properly describe such transitions.  
In multiscale schemes, residues like tryptophan are typically treated at the MM level and would therefore not contribute to the electronic spectrum.  
These results thus advocate for all-atom approaches when studying fluorescent or photoactive proteins in which additional absorbing residues may significantly contribute to the optical response.  

Recently, one of us introduced a highly efficient exciton-coupling (ExC) approach for the simulation of electronic absorption and electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra of molecular aggregates containing up to approximately 10,000 atoms.\cite{paulsEfficientExcitonCoupling2025}
The method is formulated within the sTDA framework by partitioning the system into nonoverlapping fragments and neglecting interfragment charge-transfer excitations, thereby reducing the parent eigenvalue problem to a basis of independently computed fragment excited states.
This leads to an ExC Hamiltonian that retains the accuracy of the parent sTD-DFT approach for sufficiently separated fragments while reducing the computational cost by roughly two orders of magnitude.
Applications to organic aggregates and DNA helices demonstrate the capability of the method to treat large supramolecular systems efficiently, while dielectric continuum embedding (using COSMO) and fragment-merging strategies were shown to mitigate deficiencies associated with environmental polarization and short-range intermolecular interactions in polar systems.


Together, these examples emphasize the complementary roles of ADQM and ASQM: the former enables a realistic treatment of complex chromophores in solution or flexible biomolecules by incorporating structural dynamics, whereas the latter delivers accurate spectra for comparatively rigid systems at a reduced computational cost, while preserving a fully quantum-mechanical description of the entire assembly.

\subsection{Linear response: optical rotation}

The prediction of molar optical rotation $[\Phi]_D$ (in \si{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole}, typically measured at the sodium D-line, \SI{589.3}{\nano\meter}) remains a stringent test for electronic-structure methods, owing to its pronounced sensitivity to the underlying excited-state manifold.  
A commonly used reference is the OR45 benchmark set introduced by Srebro \textit{et al.},\cite{srebroOpticalRotationCalculated2011} which reports experimental $[\Phi]_D$ values for 42 organic molecules and three transition-metal complexes.  
For the subset of 35 molecules with rotations between $-400$ and \SI{500}{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole}, TD-DFT calculations typically yield median relative deviations of 25--29\% with respect to experimental liquid-phase data, corresponding to median absolute deviations of \SI{24}{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole} (PBE0) and \SI{29}{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole} (B3LYP).  
This results in a ``zone of indeterminacy'' of approximately \SI{60}{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole}, below which both the magnitude and even the sign of the rotation may become unreliable.  
These limitations primarily arise from the sensitivity of optical rotation to Rydberg and charge-transfer states, as well as from neglected effects such as solvation, vibrational contributions, and conformational averaging.

Within this context, sTD-DFT was previously extended\cite{dewergifosseSimplifiedTimedependentDensity2020} to evaluate optical rotation using Eq.~\eqref{eq:linresp}.  
The method provides good agreement with experiment for systems dominated by valence excitations, such as extended $\pi$-conjugated helicenes (compounds 38-42 or the benchmark set), while offering speedups of one to three orders of magnitude compared to TD-DFT.  
For smaller systems with significant Rydberg character, the accuracy deteriorates, consistent with the intrinsic limitations observed in the OR45 benchmark.  
An additional technical aspect concerns gauge-origin invariance: although both length and velocity formulations can be employed, residual deviations remain when finite basis sets are used, typically below 1\% but reaching up to 30\% in challenging cases.

To assess the impact of the XsTD approximation, we revisited the OR45 benchmark (the organic molecules, see Fig.~S6) using the original XC functionals (B3LYP, PBE0, and BHHLYP), complemented by additional hybrid and range-separated hybrid functionals (M06-2X, CAM-B3LYP, and $\omega$B97X-D3), see details in the SI.  
The molar rotation $[\Phi]_\omega$ is defined as
\begin{equation*}
	[\Phi]_\omega = \overbrace{72\times 10^6\,\frac{\hbar^2\,\mathcal N_a}{c^2m_e^2}}^{6.4657\times 10^{6}} \times \frac{\omega^2\,\beta(\omega)}{100},
\end{equation*}
where $\omega$ is the incident light frequency and $\beta(\omega)$ the rotationally averaged electric--magnetic dipole polarizability, both in atomic units.  
This quantity can be evaluated in both the length (LG) and velocity (using the momentum operator, $\hat p$) formulations:
\begin{equation*}
	\beta^{LG}(\omega) = -\text{Tr}\,\frac{\Im\braket{\braket{\hat\mu^L;\hat m}}_\omega}{3\omega} \text{ or }
	\beta^{pG}(\omega) = -\text{Tr}\,\frac{\Re\braket{\braket{\hat p;\hat m}}_\omega}{3\omega^2},
\end{equation*}
with the latter being formally origin invariant.  
In practice, improved results are obtained using the modified velocity gauge (MVG),\cite{pedersenOriginInvariantCalculation2004,caricatoOriginInvariantOptical2020}
\begin{equation*}
	\beta^{MVG}(\omega) = -\,\text{Tr}\,\frac{\Re\left[\braket{\braket{\hat p;\hat m}}_\omega - \braket{\braket{\hat p;\hat m}}_0\right]}{3\omega^2}.
\end{equation*}
The resulting distributions of $[\Phi]_D$ errors, relative to experiment, are summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:or} for both LG and MVG formulations.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:or}, the XsTD-DFT predictions of $[\Phi]_D$ follow the trends obtained with the sTD-DFT scheme, confirming a good predictive capability.  
Overall, XsTD-DFT shows comparable or slightly improved MDs but somewhat larger MADs (generaly above one third of an order of magnitude).  
A detailed statistical analysis is provided in the SI (Tables~S1--S2): as in previous studies, systems dominated by $\pi \to \pi^\star$ excitations are well described, whereas molecules with significant Rydberg character remain challenging.  
B3LYP and PBE0 provide the most consistent results, while RSH functionals can offer improvements for specific subsets such as helicenes; in this context, CAM-B3LYP, as well as its simplified variants sCAM-B3LYP and XsCAM-B3LYP, emerge as competitive alternatives, although the sTD variant generally performs more consistently than XsTD, as also reflected in Fig.~\ref{fig:or}.  
No significant difference is observed between LG and MVG results, likely because the molecules were positioned close to their center of charge.

In terms of interpretation, norbornenone (molecule 20 in the OR45 set) represents a particularly challenging case for theoretical chiroptical methods due to its exceptionally large experimental molar rotation, ranging from approximately \SIrange{-1146}{-1239}{\degree\centi\meter\squared\per\deci\mole} depending on the solvent.  
This value is considerably larger than that of structurally related compounds such as camphor (21) or norbornanone (19), a phenomenon attributed to the electronic coupling between the nearly orthogonal \ce{C=C} and \ce{C=O} $\pi$ systems.  
Conventional TD-DFT calculations at the B3LYP/aug-cc-pVDZ or PBE0/aug-cc-pVDZ level reproduce the experimental values with deviations below \SI{5}{\percent}, whereas simplified sTD-DFT or XsTD-DFT approaches generally underestimate the response by roughly \SI{20}{\percent}, depending on the functional.  
Notably, XsCAM-B3LYP/aug-cc-pVDZ performs exceptionally well for this system, yielding an error of only \SI{3.6}{\percent}.

A comparative analysis using the NRO framework [Eq.~\eqref{eq:nros}] highlights the distinct electronic origins of optical rotation in these bicyclic ketones.\cite{dewergifosseUnifiedStrategyChemically2020}  
For norbornenone (Fig.~\ref{fig:nros-20}), the response is highly localized and dominated by the $\beta_{zz}$ component of the OR tensor, with the $z$-axis oriented along the \ce{C=O} bond.  
The NRO analysis indicates that the response is primarily governed by a single transition (weight 0.52) contributing \SI{-2.9}{a.u.}, corresponding to the coupling between the carbonyl $n \to \pi^\star$ excitation and the $\pi \to \pi^\star$ transition of the \ce{C=C} bond.  
Fragment-based decomposition further confirms that the interaction between the carbonyl and alkene fragments is the main driver of the enhanced chiroptical response.  
This demonstrates that NROs provide a level of interpretive power comparable to NTOs for understanding the origin of molecular optical responses.  
In contrast, camphor exhibits a more delocalized response, characterized by a large participation ratio ($PR_\mathrm{NRO} = 29.1$) and dominated by numerous low-weight Rydberg-type excitations, which complicates both interpretation and predictive modeling.
This is solved through the use of fragment-based decomposition.

\subsection{Quadratic responses}

Quadratic response properties, such as SHG, 2PA, and ESA, provide direct access to nonlinear optical behavior and higher-order light--matter interactions that are central in modern spectroscopy, photonics, and bioimaging. 
From a theoretical standpoint, their evaluation is significantly more demanding than linear-response quantities: as a result, conventional \textit{ab initio} and full TD-DFT approaches rapidly become prohibitive for realistic system sizes or extensive conformational sampling.  
Simplified quantum chemical methods therefore offer a particularly appealing route for quadratic responses at a computational cost compatible with large molecules and complex chemical environments.

A comprehensive assessment of 2PA properties with  our simplified methods was also carried out in Ref.~\citenum{maraldiEvaluatingPerformanceExact2025}, again for both the QUEST (Fig.~S5) and CT (Fig.~\ref{fig:2pa-ct}) datasets, against RI-CC2 reference data.  
Across both datasets, trends in excitation-energy MDs for 2PA-active states closely mirror those observed for 1PA-active states, with XsTD-DFT consistently improving excitation energies relative to sTD-DFT while retaining similar accuracy for $\delta^{2\mathrm{PA}}$.  
Global hybrid functionals with modest Hartree--Fock exchange, such as B3LYP and PBE0, yield the best overall agreement with RI-CC2 for 2PA strengths, with MDs below one third of an order of magnitude and reasonable MADs.  
In contrast, functionals with large Hartree--Fock exchange and range-separated hybrids tend to systematically underestimate 2PA cross sections within simplified schemes, likely due to the neglect of the exchange--correlation kernel response in quadratic properties. 
While sTD-DFT and XsTD-DFT deliver comparable MDs (and MADs) for $\log(\delta^{2\mathrm{PA}})$, XsTD-DFT offers a clear advantage for excitation energies, reducing deviations by nearly a factor of two.  
Overall, the same considerations guiding XCFs selection in conventional TD-DFT apply to XsTD-DFT, with PBE0 and B3LYP emerging as practical compromises for simultaneously reproducing RI-CC2 excitation energies and 2PA strengths, consistent with earlier TD-DFT benchmarks.

Consistent with the conclusions drawn from the 2PA benchmarks, a similarly favorable picture emerges for first hyperpolarizabilities, demonstrating that simplified approaches can reliably describe quadratic response properties.  
For small push--pull $\pi$-conjugated molecules, the original sTD-DFT scheme is known to exhibit artificially red-shifted resonance enhancements in $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$.  
To mitigate this limitation, some of use previously proposed refitting the MNOK two-electron integral parameters in order to better reproduce higher-level reference calculations.  
This strategy proved particularly effective for large systems, within the ASQM framework.  
In Ref.~\citenum{beaujeanAllAtomQuantumMechanical2021}, bacteriorhodopsin (bR, $\sim$4000 atoms) was investigated as a challenging test case, benefiting from the availability of experimental $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ spectra measured by Clays and co-workers.\cite{decoeneFluorescenceFreeSpectralDispersion2017}  
As customary, the experimental data were extrapolated to the static limit using refined two-state models; here, a simple vibronic model was employed with parameters adjusted to reproduce the experimental one-photon absorption spectrum.  
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:beta-bR}, the sTD-DFT-xTB $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ frequency dispersion quantitatively reproduces the low-energy experimental points below the two-photon resonance and closely follows the extrapolation toward the static limit.

Although response theory is expected to break down close to resonance, the agreement in the low-frequency regime is remarkable, highlighting the robustness of this parameterized simplified approach for large biomolecular systems.  
Current developments aim at extending the applicability of these methods to the resonant regime through damped response formalisms.\cite{kauczorEfficiencyAlgorithmsSolving2011,KauczorEfficient2014}

Note that within the XsTD-DFT framework, which replaces semiempirical MNOK integrals with exact one- and two-center atomic-orbital contributions, the need for reparameterization disappears altogether.  
Initial benchmark frequency-dispersion curves\cite{dewergifosseEXactIntegralSimplified2024} already show that XsTD-DFT nearly reproduces the $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ profiles obtained with full TD-DFT, in particular the position of resonance enhancements associated with charge-transfer states.  
Future work will aim at systematically assessing the improvements provided by XsTD-DFT for $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$.

The sTD-DFT framework has also proven useful for modeling ESA spectra of large molecular systems.  
A representative example is provided by PYP, whose transient absorption spectrum was measured \SI{1.5}{\pico\second} after a \SI{370}{\nano\meter} pump excitation in aqueous buffer.  
At this delay time, the experimental signal is dominated by negative contributions arising from ground-state bleaching and stimulated emission, leading to three main spectral features: an ESA band near \SI{370}{\nano\meter}, ground-state bleaching around \SI{445}{\nano\meter}, and stimulated emission centered at approximately \SI{495}{\nano\meter}.  
To interpret these observations, the ground-state absorption and the first singlet ESA spectrum of PYP were computed using the sTDA-xTB approach.  
A global energy shift of \SI{-0.5}{\electronvolt} was applied to align the calculated spectra with respect to experiment, and the transient signal was reconstructed by subtracting the ground-state absorption and a modeled stimulated emission contribution from the ESA spectrum.  
Despite the neglect of explicit vibronic structure and inhomogeneous broadening effects, the resulting transient spectrum qualitatively reproduces the main experimental features and clarifies the interplay between ESA and stimulated emission, in particular the attenuation of the stimulated emission band due to overlapping ESA transitions.

Then, the 2PA spectrum of FMN in water (Fig.~\ref{fig:2pa-fmn}) provides a clear illustration of the respective strengths and limitations of ASQM and ADQM strategies for quadratic responses.\cite{loffelsenderAllAtomQuantumMechanical2025}  
Similarly to the corresponding 1PA spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:1pa-fmn}), ASQM calculations in vacuum, performed at the RI-CC2 and sTD-DFT-xTB levels, already reproduce the position of the lowest experimental 2PA band with reasonable accuracy.  
However, they show significant discrepancies in intensity and fail to capture the shape and splitting of the higher-energy spectral features.  
Averaging over uncorrelated gas-phase snapshots provides only marginal improvement, while ADQM-Boltz.~calculations based on optimized conformers do not substantially alter the spectral profile.  
This behavior reflects the fact that 1PA and 2PA probe the same underlying electronic transitions and are therefore only weakly sensitive to static structural disorder.  
In contrast, the ADQM-MD protocol, combining sTD-DFT-xTB with explicit solvation spheres extracted from molecular dynamics simulations, yields a marked improvement over ASQM.  
In particular, it accurately reproduces both peak positions and the pronounced inhomogeneous broadening of the second 2PA band observed experimentally.  
When sufficiently large solvation environments are included, the ADQM-MD spectra not only outperform vacuum and continuum-based ASQM models but also compare favorably with previous QM/MM calculations, capturing solvent-induced splitting and intensity redistribution at a fraction of the computational cost.

The ADQM approach can be naturally extended to compute $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ by averaging over ensembles of structures extracted from MD simulations, as conformational fluctuations can have a pronounced impact on the NLO response of flexible systems.  
A clear illustration is provided in Ref.~\citenum{seibertDynamicStructuralEffects2020} for Gramicidin-A through its model peptide KWWKWWK.  
For this system, individual MD snapshots yield $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ values that vary by up to a factor of six, highlighting the strong sensitivity of the first hyperpolarizability to subtle structural variations, such as the relative orientation of the indole side chains (Fig.~\ref{fig:beta-kwwkwwk}).  
When averaged over the entire trajectory, however, the resulting frequency dispersion of $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ is only moderately reduced (by about 2--22\%) compared to that of the lowest-energy conformer.  
This indicates that ensemble averaging smooths the most extreme fluctuations while preserving the overall NLO response of the system.  
In this case, comparisons between ADQM-MD and ADQM-Boltz.~results show similar trends, suggesting that relatively short simulations can already capture the dominant conformational contributions for systems characterized by shallow potential energy surfaces.

Another application of the ADQM-MD strategy to SHG focused on self-assembled fluorescent organic nanoparticles (FONs) composed of dipolar $\pi$-conjugated push--pull chromophores.\cite{lescosSelfassemblingStructureNonlinear2021}  
Classical MD simulations (250--300~ns) of systems containing 100 dyes and 46\,850 water molecules revealed aggregation patterns dominated by parallel $\pi$-stacking of triphenylamine units, leading to enhanced dipolar character.  
The SHG response was evaluated using 
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
	\item TD-DFT/M06-2X/6-311+G(d) tensor summation,
	\item the same protocol at the sTD-DFT-vTB level (where some basis sets where removed), and 
	\item full sTD-DFT-vTB calculations on the entire nanoparticle,
\end{inparaenum}
with the latter showing an enhancement of $\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}$ at \SI{1064}{\nano\meter} by a factor of $\sim 3$ relative to the additive model.  
This behavior was traced back to aggregation-induced low-energy charge-transfer states, although quantitative agreement with experiment remained limited by the nanoparticle size and the approximate treatment of the environment.  

More recently, a similar strategy was extended to large, disordered supramolecular aggregates (azobenzene self-assembled monolayers, SAMs) through an automated tuning of the MNOK parameters ($\gamma_J$ and $\gamma_K$) within the sTD-DFT framework.\cite{huggetSimplifiedTimeDependentDFT2026a}  
Using ground-state orbitals from either DFT or xTB, the resulting parametrization was shown to be transferable from isolated molecules to large clusters representative of azobenzene self-assembled monolayers extracted from MD simulations.  
In this context, sTD-DFT reproduces TD-DFT NLO responses while enabling fully quantum-mechanical calculations on significantly larger systems.  
In particular, while TD-DFT is restricted to small clusters (radius $<\SI{9}{\angstrom}$), optimally tuned sTD-DFT, while following the trend of TD-DFT, enables a systematic exploration of larger environments.  
Applications to trans-azobenzene SAMs (Fig.~\ref{fig:beta-sams}) reveal a marked decrease of $\braket{\beta_{\mathrm{HRS}}/N_{\mathrm{AZO}}}$ with increasing cluster size, highlighting the strong impact of aggregation effects on the SHG response.  
For cis isomers, a less regular size dependence is observed, which can be attributed to structural distortions inducing near-resonant contributions at the second harmonic.

\section{Conclusions and perspectives}

In summary, the \texttt{std2} package provides an efficient and modular framework for simplified quantum chemical approaches to excited states and response properties.  
By introducing controlled approximations, such as the neglect of the exchange--correlation kernel and simplified treatments of two-electron integrals, (X)sTD-DFT enables routine calculations on systems comprising thousands of atoms, thereby bridging the gap between computational cost and predictive capability.  
Benchmark comparisons against higher-level methods (e.g., RI-CC2) show that XsTD-DFT accurately reproduces excitation energies and oscillator strengths for valence-dominated transitions, while improving upon sTD-DFT for charge-transfer states, with typical speedups of two to three orders of magnitude.  
The versatility of the framework is further demonstrated through applications to a wide range of spectroscopic observables, including 1PA, 2PA, ECD, OR, as well as nonlinear responses such as SHG and ESA.  
Moreover, the use of ASQM and ADQM schemes, in particular ADQM-MD, enables the inclusion of environmental and dynamical effects, which are essential for realistic simulations of complex (bio)molecular systems.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain.  
In particular, Rydberg and doubly excited states are less reliably described due to the underlying ZDO approximations.  
Although XsTD-DFT reduces the dependence on empirical parameters, certain quadratic response properties may still benefit from improved integral treatments or targeted reparameterization, especially in resonant regimes.  
Furthermore, the overall accuracy remains sensitive to the choice of exchange--correlation functional: global hybrids such as B3LYP and PBE0 generally provide balanced performance, whereas range-separated hybrids may be advantageous for specific systems or properties.

Note also that the current \texttt{std2} implementation remains limited for very large systems (if $E_{thr}$ gets large), particularly for quadratic response properties and the evaluation of their residues, due to the explicit construction and diagonalization of large matrices.  
These limitations will be alleviated in the future by adopting iterative eigensolvers, such as the Davidson method, which would enable a more efficient treatment of selected excited states and significantly reduce both memory and computational requirements.\cite{kauczorEfficiencyAlgorithmsSolving2011,KauczorEfficient2014}
Preliminary implementations of such iterative approaches for linear and quadratic response properties and residues have already been developed and show promising results, with significant reductions in computational cost.

Future developments will focus on extending both the robustness and the scope of the approach.  
Promising directions include:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Improving the quality of ground-state wavefunctions, while reducing the computational cost, for instance through integration with newly developed tight-binding approaches such as g-xTB, to enhance the reliability of subsequent excited-state calculations.
	\item Incorporating damped response formalisms to enable a more robust and physically sound treatment of resonant nonlinear properties.
	Preliminary tests show that the near resonance region is well described by this new addition.
	\item Developing a new  sQC approaches, called $\eta$XsTD-DFT, inspired by recent low-scaling TD-DFT strategies (e.g., TD-DFT-ris), to further reduce computational cost while retaining accuracy for targeted classes of excitations and properties.
	Preliminary tests show that it works surprisingly well.
	\item Advancing parallelization and high-performance computing implementations to efficiently tackle increasingly large molecular systems.
	\item Extending the methodology to additional spectroscopic observables, including phosphorescence, Raman scattering, and higher-order nonlinear response properties.
	\item Extending the Xs methodology to the treatment of electron correlation, such as multi-reference or MP2 corrections.
\end{enumerate}

Overall, simplified approaches such as those implemented in \texttt{std2} are expected to play an increasingly important role in predictive spectroscopy, enabling quantum-mechanical investigations of complex molecular systems across chemistry, materials science, and biology.
